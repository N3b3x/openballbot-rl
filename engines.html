Does the result depend on the engine?

Physics engines

Every number on this site comes from MuJoCo on the CPU. Before moving the study to a faster engine we asked two questions: does the same robot with the same controller behave the same way, and is it actually faster? Two side experiments answer them. Simulation only; no hardware claims.

Verdicts

One engine is a plausible accelerator, the other is a cross-check

MuJoCo-Warp (GPU): go with caveats

Same contact model as our CPU engine. Under the LQR controller the speed ratios agree to within 0.01 and 94.6 % of speed runs and 96.1 % of push runs agree on fall versus no fall across 17 designs. About 260 times the throughput of one CPU process at 4096 parallel worlds. A small GPU PPO test then trained a policy that transfers to the CPU engine (below). Not yet tested: sensors, terrain, the motor model, per-world randomisation.

Isaac Sim (PhysX): no-go as the main engine

It installs and runs here, but PhysX has no anisotropic friction, so the wheels needed a fragile roller-ring emulation. It reproduces nominal tracking and the contact-angle ranking but not the failure boundaries: at 1.1 times speed MuJoCo falls and PhysX holds. No large speedup was shown. Useful later as an independent cross-check.

What the gap means. Warp reproduces the headline behaviour, but its trajectory error is 25 to 100 times the CPU version-to-version spread. The likely cause is float32 plus solver effects amplified by the unstable robot (open loop it e-folds in about 0.16 s); this was not isolated. GPU runs are not bit-reproducible, and marginal designs flip individual runs, so any GPU-trained policy would need CPU validation.

17-design sweep

Designs vary contact angle, scale, mass distribution, rotor inertia, motor torque and wheel friction (the two friction variants were made for this test). The design ranking is preserved: tall-centre-of-mass and 1.5x-scale robots are robust, low-centre, high-ball-mass and light-ball designs are fragile, and the half-scale robot fails on both engines. The largest disagreement is the 0.25 friction variant (CPU 4 of 4 falls at 0.4, Warp 1 of 4); the cause is not known. Per-design table: outputs/study/mjx_port/sweep_cpu_vs_warp_wide.csv.

Training a policy on the GPU engine

A small PPO test ran the whole loop on the GPU: physics, observations, reward, resets and the PPO update. 60 million steps took 7 minutes (about 140,000 steps per second); the CPU runs reach 5 million in hours. The policy was then evaluated on the CPU engine with the same protocol as every other controller.

Limits: one seed, no mass, friction or damping randomisation on the GPU yet, flat floor, push test shows Warp harsher than CPU for reasons not yet understood. Code: experiments/warp_ppo/.

Scale-up: seven designs, three nominal seeds

The same GPU recipe with full per-world randomisation (mass, friction, damping, torque, pushes, latency, noise) was repeated for the nominal design with three seeds and for six other designs. Each policy was checked on both engines with the study protocol. Every design that balances on Warp balances on the CPU engine, and the one that fails (1.5 times scale) fails on both, as it did with CPU-trained PPO.

    Where the physics models differ

    How to read it. The MJCF model was converted with the official converter. Anisotropic ball-wheel friction is not available in PhysX, so each wheel was emulated with a ring of 16 passive rollers. Reduced protocol (2 headings, 4 pushes per level) for PhysX run time; MuJoCo re-run on the same protocol. Source: experiments/isaac_port/REPORT.md.

    • Failure boundaries differ. At 1.1 times speed MuJoCo falls (all runs in 8 of 9 designs) while PhysX holds, with a ratio near 1.1. Fall counts agree in only 1 of 9 designs at 1.1 and 7 of 9 at 0.8. In the 1x push PhysX falls more in 5 of 9 designs and less in none.
    • The contact-angle ranking is reproduced: the 66 degree design survives pushes and the 41 degree design does not.
    • The emulation is fragile. For the same run 2 of 4 roller-ring configurations held balance and 2 fell; a naive import (isotropic friction, capsule wheels) loses balance after 0.39 s.
    • Not finished in PhysX: 8 of the 17 designs (including the half-scale and light-ball ones). Friction forces from the PhysX contact report read about 15 times lower than MuJoCo's, so only normal forces were compared.
    • Speed: at 4096 environments about 15,000 control steps per second, roughly 10 times one MuJoCo process, with a 35 to 59 ms floor per physics step that may be contention with training (not resolved).
    • Cost on disk: the install is a 7.5 GB download and a 16 GB environment.
    Reading. PhysX is a second, independent engine for sanity checks, not a drop-in replacement: the behaviours our study cares about (where the controller falls) are exactly where it disagrees. The cheaper way to accelerate is MuJoCo-Warp, which keeps the MuJoCo contact model.
