LQR balance control

A balance controller is a weighted sum of four measurements. This lesson shows where its weights come from, what each one does, and where a linear controller stops being enough.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • name the four state variables and say what each gain does;
  • explain what LQR optimises and how Q and R trade accuracy against effort;
  • read a pole plot and recognise a loop that is unstable, ringing or saturated.

3.1State feedback in plain words

Lesson 1 gave the model: four numbers describe one plane of the robot, the state x = (ball angle, tilt, ball rate, tilt rate). If we measure all four, the simplest controller is a weighted sum that sets the motor torque:

τ = −K x = −(K1 φ + K2 θ + K3 (φ' − φ'ref) + K4 θ')

Each gain has a plain meaning. K2 (tilt) is a spring: lean this far, push this hard to bring the ball under you. K4 (tilt rate) is a damper that stops the correction from overshooting. K3 (ball rate) links the ball's speed to the command, so the robot tracks a velocity instead of just standing. The position gain K1 is near zero on purpose (its weight is 10-6): the controller tracks velocity, and holding a position is the job of the layers above (lessons 4 and 5).

How do you pick the gains? LQR (linear quadratic regulator) chooses them by solving an optimisation: minimise the sum over time of xT Q x + τT R τ. Q says how much you dislike each kind of error, R how much you dislike effort. The weights used in this project are Q = diag(10-6, 50, 1, 1) and R = 1, at the 10 ms control period, giving gains of about (−73.9, −4.2, −11.2) for tilt, ball rate and tilt rate on the nominal robot.

Try it

Reset the gains. (1) Push at 40 N, then reduce the wheel torque limit until the robot falls. (2) Put the tilt-rate gain at ×0.3 and describe what the closed-loop poles do. (3) Set the effort weight R to 10 and the tilt weight to 1000.

Show the answer

(1) A single push needs only a modest torque; the limit at which it fails is visible in the widget (this planar model, one push at one instant). It matches the design sweep qualitatively: the weak-motor design (6 N m) mostly balances (13 percent of standing robots fell), while 3 N m falls about half the time even standing (lesson 8, S119). (2) With too little damping the poles move toward the imaginary axis and the response rings. (3) Heavier tilt weights and cheaper effort raise the gains and speed the recovery; heavier effort weights lower them and the robot reacts lazily. LQR turns "how much do I care" into gains; it does not tell you what to care about.

3.2What LQR cannot do alone

The loop above is the "model-based" half of the controller (blue throughout the site). It balances, rejects pushes within the motor limits, and tracks velocity commands slowly and exactly. Its limits come from the model it was built on: it is linear, it knows nothing about friction, slip, latency, terrain, sensor noise, or the 11 different robots the project later trains on. On the flat goal test (arrive at a point 1.2 m away and stay), the LQR alone fell in 32.4 percent of simulated trials, against 6.8 percent for the full controller of lesson 4 (S114 addendum). The worst case is the light-ball design, where the LQR alone fell in 96 percent of trials.

That gap is the opening for learning, but not for replacing the LQR: the learned part is added on top of it. Lesson 4 shows why.

Check yourself

In the widget, you set the tilt gain to 0. What do you expect, and why?

Why

Without the tilt term the controller has nothing that pushes the ball back under a leaning body. A damper alone slows the fall but cannot reverse it, and the open-loop pole at 4.79 1/s remains in the right half plane (the pole plot shows it).

SummaryKey takeaways

  • State feedback sets the torque from tilt, tilt rate and ball rate; the tilt gain is the spring that makes the unstable pole stable.
  • LQR picks the gains by minimising a weighted sum of error and effort; the weights are the design choice (Q = diag(1e-6, 50, 1, 1), R = 1).
  • A linear model-based loop balances and tracks speed exactly but fails often at goals (32 percent falls alone) on a randomised simulator.
  • Torque limits matter: the loop works only while the motors can supply what it asks.
