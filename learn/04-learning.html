Adding learning

The recommended controller is an LQR with a small learned correction. This lesson shows what the correction adds, why it is kept small, and how its size is scheduled.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • describe the residual policy and write the rule that combines the LQR and the network;
  • explain the tracking against robustness trade-off and why 0.25 is the knee;
  • describe adaptive authority and the goal dead band, and why one seed was not enough to justify the latter.

4.1Why not just learn everything?

Reinforcement learning, here PPO (proximal policy optimisation), trains a neural network by trial and error in simulation. Each 10 ms the network sees a 25-number observation (tilt, rates, velocities, the last action, the command, the goal), the last 8 steps of history (80 ms) and a 6-number description of which robot it is driving, and outputs three wheel commands. The network has three hidden layers of 512 units. It trains for 200 million simulated steps on 4096 robots in parallel on one GPU, in MuJoCo-Warp.

A policy trained from scratch can balance, and with the right training it is the most robust controller on the robots it was trained on (S82). But it has no physics built in, so it cannot extrapolate: on an unseen robot, 1.5 times larger than any in training, pure learning succeeded in 9 percent of goal trials, while the LQR-based controllers still reached 71 percent (S85-R).

4.2The residual: learn only what the LQR gets wrong

The deployed controller is a residual policy. Each tick the LQR computes its action u, the network computes a correction a in [−1, 1], and the applied action is

applied = clip( u + rs · a , −1, 1 )  // rs is the residual authority

With rs = 0 the robot is the plain LQR of lesson 3. With rs = 1 the network can override the LQR completely. In between, the network can only nudge. This is what makes the combination robust: the physics-based part gives a safe default for any robot and any situation the network has not seen, and the learned part handles friction, latency, slip, pushes and steps that the linear model ignores.

4.3Authority is a dial between tracking and recovery

How much authority should the network get? The study mapped the curve (S82, S83) and found a trade-off. A small residual keeps the LQR's exact speed tracking and still gains most of the robustness. A larger one gains a little more robustness under pushes but follows commands less well, because the network pulls the robot away from what the LQR would do. Pure learning is at the far end.

0.25 is a reasonable answer: exact tracking and most of the robustness. The chart hides seed noise (the ranges are over only one or two runs), the effects on goal arrival and holding (S111: authority 0.15 fell three times as often at goals as 0.25, 0.30 against 0.11 to 0.12) and the robot design. Which is exactly why the next step is not a single number but a rule.

4.4Adaptive authority and the goal dead band

Instead of one level, the deployed controller changes authority with the situation (S112). It uses 0.15 while cruising (exact tracking) and rises at once to 0.3 whenever the robot needs the network's help: when the command changes faster than 0.3 m/s per second, when the command is near zero (the robot is stopping or holding), or when the body tilts beyond 4 degrees. After the need passes it decays back over about 0.5 s. Training with this rule gave the lowest training fall rate of the study (0.0016, S112).

The first version had a flaw. Near a goal the command goes to zero, the rule raised authority, and one training seed hunted around the goal, reversing its command 4.9 times per run. The fix (S113) is a dead band: the near-zero term only counts while the ball still moves faster than 0.05 m/s. Then a later finding corrected the diagnosis: the hunting was one unlucky seed, not a property of the recipe (S113). The dead band stays because it is harmless and removes a seed-dependent failure at no measured cost.

4.5Domain randomisation across 11 designs

A policy trained on one robot in one world memorises it. So every training world is different: domain randomisation varies the masses (15 percent), floor friction (20 percent), wheel damping (0.07 to 0.15), motor torque (15 percent), the observation noise and a one-step latency (20 percent probability), and applies random pushes of 5 to 20 N every 2 to 4 s (configs/env/dz_nom.yaml). The randomisation range is also widened during training (to 1.75 times in the study recipe) so the evaluation range sits inside what was seen.

On top of that, the 6-number design vector tells the policy which of 11 robot designs it is in: the nominal one, a high and a low centre of mass, a heavy and a light ball, a heavy and a light body, a large rotor inertia, weak motors, and two wheel-contact angles. The same network drives all of them.

Check yourself

The residual policy is trained with authority 0.3 and the robot is then put on a ball of a size never seen in training. Which part of the controller still does something sensible?

Why

The LQR gains come from the model of the actual robot, so they stay meaningful as designs change (S85-R: LQR-based controllers 0.61 to 0.71 on the unseen 1.5x robot). The learned part has no online adaptation and can be wrong on an unfamiliar ball: S119 found a 12 cm ball failed 69 percent with the learned controller but only 2 percent with the LQR alone. The residual limits the damage; it does not remove it.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • PPO trains a network by trial and error in simulation; on its own it does not transfer to an unseen robot (0.09 on a 1.5x robot).
  • The residual keeps the LQR as a safe base and lets the network nudge it: applied = clip(lqr + rs a).
  • Authority is a dial: low is exact tracking, high is more robust to pushes; 0.25 is the knee.
  • Adaptive authority uses 0.15 while cruising and 0.3 when needed (command change, near-zero command, tilt), with a dead band at the goal.
  • Domain randomisation over masses, friction, torque, latency, noise and pushes, plus 11 designs, makes one network fit many robots.
