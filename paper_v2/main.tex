\title{Derived Dynamics Meet Learned Control:\\
Omnidirectional Velocity Tracking for a Small Ballbot}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Nebiyu Tadesse}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Johns Hopkins University\\Working draft; updated as experiments complete}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A ballbot balances on a single ball and moves by leaning, so even ``drive left at 0.5\,m/s'' requires pushing the ball the wrong way first, holding a precise lean while accelerating, and never letting the ball escape. We study this velocity-tracking problem for a small three-omniwheel ballbot from two directions and connect them. \emph{From first principles}, we derive the planar Euler--Lagrange dynamics including the omniwheel transmission and reflected rotor inertia, prove three structural properties (an unstable pole at $4.79\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, a non-minimum-phase zero at $\sqrt{m_a g l/(I_a+m_a l^2+m_a l r)}=5.78\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$, and a lean--acceleration law $\theta=\kappa a$ with $\kappa=0.176$\,rad per m/s$^2$ that is independent of the drive), and design an LQR controller on the derived model. \emph{From data}, we train proximal-policy-optimisation (PPO) policies over nine stages and 25 configurations, documenting a ball-ejection reward exploit, a failure of reward shaping, and the discovery that the training command distribution, not the reward form, limited tracking (speed ratio at 1\,m/s raised from 0.31 to 0.91 by capping the spin range; 0.95 on a second seed). We then test the theory on both controllers in the full MuJoCo simulator. A torque-free momentum identity of the derived model holds on all trajectories (slope 0.88--0.93, $R^2$ up to 0.82); the learned policies, trained only from reward, obey the derived lean law (91\% and 83\% of $\kappa$) and exhibit the predicted non-minimum-phase reversal. Head-to-head, the model-based LQR tracks 0.25--0.75\,m/s within 4\%, is heading-invariant and 50$\times$ smoother, while the learned policy survives the nonlinear regime (1\,m/s, turning while driving) where the LQR falls. This complementarity motivates a residual policy on top of the LQR. \emph{At scale}, we move the learning loop onto a GPU physics engine and, on the corrected engine (a late-found error, stale subtree mass under mass randomisation, invalidated all earlier randomised results), compare pure learning, an LQR and a bounded learned residual on an LQR base. On a clean flat world pure learning reaches only about 60\,\% of the commanded speed while the LQR is 9--15 times more accurate and the residual keeps that accuracy (error 6--12\,\% of the command up to 0.9\,m/s); on a goal task across ten terrain kinds the residual succeeds in 0.58 of worlds against 0.44 for pure learning, and two seeds agree to about 0.02. Training over wider randomisation changes almost nothing, and no training recipe improves success under pushes and pulls (about 0.3) or the tile velocity task. A fall diagnosis shows that the body derails off the ball at about 15$^\circ$ of tilt with torque to spare; a passive ball retainer (arrestors after ETH Zurich's Rezero, our own design, adding no torque about the ball centre) raises goal success with pushes and pulls to about 0.47 and cuts tile velocity falls from 0.53 to about 0.32 (two seeds), and with it the learned authority sets a trade-off between tracking and robustness: we recommend the LQR plus residual at authority 0.25 trained with the retainer, which tracks speed exactly and reaches the goal in 0.42 to 0.44 of worlds with pushes and pulls (0.29 to 0.30 without the retainer) on three seeds. The remaining terrain falls are set mainly by obstacle size and match a traction limit set by ball radius and friction (a larger ball crosses them; a perfect height map given to the policy does not help); on the corrected contact model they also rise with approach speed, but a terrain-aware speed layer that slows the robot before a feature reduces them by only 0.03 to 0.05; routing around what cannot be crossed and a larger ball are what work. Where obstacles can be avoided, terrain sensing is decisive: a planner on top of the frozen recommended controller cuts falls from \gv{nav26.blind.fall} to \gv{nav26.oracle.fall} with a perfect map, and smooth sensor-based navigation with a modest forward time-of-flight sensor reaches \gv{nav26.tof.success} of goals behind unclimbable blocks, within \gv{nav26.tof_gap} of the perfect map; the remaining failures are push recoveries near obstacles, a balance-controller limit, and training with the planner in the loop changes nothing on two seeds. Our final recommendation adds a motion-reference layer between planner and controller, trained together with an adaptive residual authority (0.15 while cruising, 0.3 when manoeuvring) and a goal dead band: over three seeds it reaches \gv{mrf.g34.goal.1.u.min} to \gv{mrf.g34.goal.1.u.max} of terrain goals undisturbed and \gv{mrf.g34.goal.1.d.min} to \gv{mrf.g34.goal.1.d.max} under pushes and pulls, and with the ToF sensor it reaches \gv{nm.g34.tof18.success} of navigation goals with about a quarter less heading wobble; a higher tracking reward, more training share for hard designs, twice the training and harder training pushes do not improve on it, and the remaining limits are physical (a light ball, a heavy body, weak motors, and sustained pulls above about 3\,N). On two unseen robot designs the LQR base carries a policy (0.61 to 0.71 on a larger robot) while pure learning collapses (0.09 to 0.15); a tenth-torque design is motor limited for every controller. Three disturbance-focused variants (higher fall penalty, push curriculum) did not help, and on held-out terrain the goal success drops only slightly. A head-mounted depth sensor gives no gain over a blind control (one seed each). All results are in simulation; we make no hardware claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Ballbots are omnidirectional, have a footprint the size of their ball and move with a fluid, lifelike quality~\cite{lauwers2006,nagarajan2014}, which makes them attractive for small character-like home robots. The control problem is hard for structural reasons: the system is underactuated (three wheel torques for a body and a ball with five planar degrees of freedom), open-loop unstable, and non-minimum phase, because the ball must first be pushed backward to tip the body forward. Classical controllers linearise the dynamics and stack an LQR or PID balance loop under a velocity loop~\cite{nagarajan2014,fankhauser2010,abdelrahim2025}; learning-based controllers promise to handle the non-linear, contact-rich regime without hand-derived models~\cite{zhou2022,salehi2025}. The two are rarely compared on the same robot, and it is rarely asked whether a learned policy behaves the way the physics says it must.

Our earlier report~\cite{tadesse2025report} reproduced the PPO pipeline of~\cite{salehi2025} and stated the Lagrangian form $M(q)\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\dot q+G(q)=\tau$ without deriving it. This paper derives that model for a specific robot, extracts testable predictions from it, builds a model-based controller, and uses both to analyse learned controllers. We focus on the interface every higher layer will use: \emph{track a commanded planar velocity and yaw rate, in any direction, smoothly, while staying on the ball.}

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A planar Euler--Lagrange model of a three-omniwheel ballbot with explicit omniwheel transmission and reflected rotor inertia, with parameters extracted from the simulator, and three structural results: instability, a closed-form non-minimum-phase zero, and a drive-independent lean--acceleration law with a torque-free momentum identity (\S\ref{sec:model}).
\item Validation of the derived model against the full 3-D MuJoCo simulation, and an LQR controller designed purely on the derived model that balances and tracks the simulated robot without simulator tuning (\S\ref{sec:lqr}).
\item A documented learning study: a ball-ejection exploit under relaxed limits, the failure of reward shaping, and evidence that infeasible commands in the training distribution suppress learning of feasible ones (\S\ref{sec:learned}).
\item A theory-versus-learning analysis showing that PPO policies rediscover the derived lean law and the non-minimum-phase reversal, and a head-to-head benchmark that motivates residual learning on top of the model-based controller (\S\ref{sec:theory}).
\item A GPU-resident training pipeline validated against the CPU engine, with the exact observation, reward, randomisation, terrain, design and evaluation definitions (\S\ref{sec:gpu}), the clean flat-world comparison of pure learning, LQR and LQR plus residual, and an account of a late-found engine error (stale subtree mass under mass randomisation) that invalidated the randomised results (\S\ref{sec:engines}, \S\ref{sec:gpuresults}).
\item A bounded residual on the LQR base that is accurate up to 0.9\,m/s on the flat world on two seeds, with its flat-world authority sweep; goal and robustness results are pending on the corrected engine (\S\ref{sec:gpuresults}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\textbf{Ballbot modelling and control.} The CMU ballbot~\cite{lauwers2006,nagarajan2014} established planar decoupling of the linearised 3-D dynamics and LQR/PID balance with an outer trajectory loop. Three-omniwheel designs with planar models and LQR follow the same approach~\cite{fankhauser2010,abdelrahim2025,xiao2023}; higher-level planners such as topology-informed MPC~\cite{mavrogiannis2021} and constrained path integrals~\cite{carius2022} assume an accurate velocity interface. The non-minimum-phase character of wheeled inverted pendulums, and the resulting bandwidth limits, are classical~\cite{astrom2008}. \textbf{Learning ballbot control.} Zhou \emph{et al.}~\cite{zhou2022} learn balancing; Salehi~\cite{salehi2025} drives over uneven terrain in a single commanded direction with PPO and depth perception, and provides the simulator we build on. \textbf{Command tracking with RL.} Randomised velocity commands with dense tracking rewards are standard for legged robots~\cite{hwangbo2019,lee2020,rudin2022}. \textbf{Smoothness, robustness and hybrid control.} Action-rate regularisation~\cite{mysore2021}, domain randomisation~\cite{tobin2017,peng2018} and residual policies on top of model-based controllers~\cite{johannink2019,silver2018} are established; we test them on a non-minimum-phase balancer. \textbf{Specification gaming.} Our ball-ejection exploit is an instance of reward misspecification~\cite{amodei2016}.

\section{System and Notation}\label{sec:system}
The MuJoCo~\cite{todorov2012} robot (Fig.~\ref{fig:paths}) has a cylindrical body with low ballast, a solid ball, and three omniwheels at $120^\circ$ azimuth spacing whose contact points lie $51.2^\circ$ from the ball's vertical. Omniwheels are modelled as capsules with anisotropic contact friction (0.001 along the roller direction, 1.0 across it); a contact-frame correction makes the low-friction direction consistent on all three wheels~\cite{salehi2025}. Actuators are torque motors with rotor inertia (armature) $J_w$ and viscous damping $d_w$. Physics runs at 500\,Hz; controllers act at 100\,Hz. \section{First-Principles Model}\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Omniwheel transmission}
Let $\bm\Omega\in\R^3$ be the angular velocity of the ball relative to the body, expressed in the body frame. Wheel $i$ has spin axis $\bm a_i$, effective radius $r_{w}$ at the contact, contact point $\bm p_i$ on the ball (relative to its centre), and rim direction $\bm t_i=\bm a_i\times(\bm p_i-\bm c_i)/r_w$ at the contact, where $\bm c_i$ is the wheel centre. No-slip in the driven direction (the passive rollers absorb the orthogonal component) requires $\omega_{w,i} r_w=(\bm\Omega\times\bm p_i)\cdot\bm t_i$, hence
\begin{equation}
\bm\omega_w = W\bm\Omega,\qquad W_{i,:}=\tfrac{1}{r_w}(\bm p_i\times\bm t_i)^{\!\top}.\label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
By virtual power, wheel torques $\bm\tau_w$ exert the torque $\bm T=W^{\!\top}\bm\tau_w$ on the ball relative to the body. For this robot $W$ is invertible, so any desired ball torque is realised by the allocation $\bm\tau_w=W^{-\top}\bm T$. In the pitch plane ($\bm\Omega=\Omega_y\hat{\bm y}$) the wheel rates are $\bm n\,\Omega_y$ with $\bm n=W_{:,2}=(3.61,-1.11,-2.50)$, and the rotors contribute kinetic energy $\tfrac12 J_w\lVert\bm n\rVert^2\Omega_y^2$. The wheels therefore act as a single reflected inertia and damper,
\begin{equation}
J_{\mathrm{eff}}=J_w\lVert\bm n\rVert^2,\qquad d_{\mathrm{eff}}=d_w\lVert\bm n\rVert^2,\label{eq:Jeff}
\end{equation}
and $J_{\mathrm{eff}}=0.1025$\,kg\,m$^2$ is 188 times the ball's own inertia: \emph{the drive, not the ball, dominates the rotational inertia seen by the controller.}

\subsection{Planar Euler--Lagrange dynamics}
Following the planar decoupling of~\cite{nagarajan2014,fankhauser2010}, consider one vertical plane with generalised coordinates $q=(\varphi,\theta)$: the absolute ball rotation angle and the absolute body tilt. Rolling without slip gives the ball position $x=r\varphi$, and the body COM is at $(x+l\sin\theta,\ l\cos\theta)$. The wheels turn with the relative angle $\varphi-\theta$. The kinetic and potential energies are
\begin{align}
T &= \tfrac12(m_k r^2+I_k)\dot\varphi^2 + \tfrac12 m_a\big[(r\dot\varphi+l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta)^2+(l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta)^2\big]\nonumber\\
&\quad+\tfrac12 I_a\dot\theta^2 + \tfrac12 J_{\mathrm{eff}}(\dot\varphi-\dot\theta)^2,\\
V &= m_a g l\cos\theta.
\end{align}
The motor torque $\tau$ and wheel damping act on the relative coordinate, so the generalised forces are $Q=(\tau-d_{\mathrm{eff}}(\dot\varphi-\dot\theta))\,(1,-1)^{\!\top}$. The Euler--Lagrange equations $\frac{d}{dt}\partial_{\dot q}L-\partial_q L=Q$ with $L=T-V$ give the form stated in~\cite{tadesse2025report},
\begin{equation}
M(q)\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + D\dot q + G(q) = B\tau,\label{eq:eom}
\end{equation}
with $B=(1,-1)^{\!\top}$, $D=d_{\mathrm{eff}}\begin{psmallmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{psmallmatrix}$, $G(q)=(0,\,-m_a g l\sin\theta)^{\!\top}$, Coriolis term $C(q,\dot q)\dot q=(-m_a l r\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2,\ 0)^{\!\top}$, and
\begin{equation}
M(q)=\begin{pmatrix}
I_k+J_{\mathrm{eff}}+(m_a+m_k)r^2 & m_a l r\cos\theta-J_{\mathrm{eff}}\\
m_a l r\cos\theta-J_{\mathrm{eff}} & I_a+J_{\mathrm{eff}}+m_a l^2
\end{pmatrix}.\label{eq:M}
\end{equation}
The derivation is reproduced symbolically by \texttt{ballbot\_model.py}. Linearising about the upright equilibrium with state $s=(\varphi,\theta,\dot\varphi,\dot\theta)$ gives $\dot s=As+B_s\tau$ with
\begin{equation}
A=\begin{psmallmatrix}0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&1\\0&11.53&-12.35&12.35\\0&27.31&6.56&-6.56\end{psmallmatrix},\quad
B_s=\begin{psmallmatrix}0\\0\\6.02\\-3.20\end{psmallmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The 3-D linearisation decouples into two such planes plus a yaw channel~\cite{nagarajan2014}.

\subsection{Structural properties}
Let $M_0=M(0)$ with entries $M_{11},M_{12},M_{22}$, and $\Delta=\det M_0$.

\begin{proposition}[Open-loop instability]\label{prop:unstable}
With $d_{\mathrm{eff}}=0$ the linearised plant has poles $\{0,0,\pm\lambda\}$ with $\lambda=\sqrt{m_a g l\,M_{11}/\Delta}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
The characteristic polynomial of~\eqref{eq:eom} linearised is $\det(M_0s^2+K)$ with $K=\mathrm{diag}(0,-m_agl)$, i.e.\ $s^2(\Delta s^2-m_agl\,M_{11})$.
\end{proof}
Numerically $\lambda=5.23\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$; with damping the unstable pole is $4.79\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (time constant 0.21\,s), so an unactuated lean grows $e$-fold every 0.21\,s.

\begin{proposition}[Non-minimum phase]\label{prop:nmp}
The transfer function from motor torque to ball position has a real zero pair $\pm z$ with
\begin{equation}
z=\sqrt{\frac{m_a g l}{I_a+m_a l^2+m_a l r}},
\end{equation}
independent of $J_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$. The zero at $+z$ makes the system non-minimum phase.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
By Cramer's rule on $(M_0s^2+Ds+K)\hat q=B\hat\tau$, the numerator of $\hat\varphi/\hat\tau$ is $(M_{22}+M_{12})s^2-m_agl$ (the $J_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$ terms cancel because both enter through the relative coordinate), and $M_{22}+M_{12}=I_a+m_al^2+m_alr$.
\end{proof}
Numerically $z=5.78\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$. Physically, to accelerate forward the controller must first tip the body forward, which by reaction drives the ball \emph{backward}; any controller, learned or not, must show this reversal, and the zero bounds the achievable tracking bandwidth~\cite{astrom2008}.

\begin{proposition}[Momentum identity and lean--acceleration law]\label{prop:lean}
Adding the two rows of~\eqref{eq:eom} eliminates $\tau$ and $d_{\mathrm{eff}}$:
\begin{equation}
(M_{11}+M_{12})\ddot\varphi+(M_{12}+M_{22})\ddot\theta-m_alr\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2=m_agl\sin\theta.\label{eq:identity}
\end{equation}
Under quasi-static lean ($\ddot\theta=\dot\theta=0$) the ball acceleration $a=r\ddot\varphi$ and the lean satisfy $\sin\theta=\kappa a$ with
\begin{equation}
\kappa=\frac{(m_a+m_k)r^2+I_k+m_alr}{m_a g l r}.\label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Row sums of $M(q)$ are $M_{11}+M_{12}=I_k+(m_a+m_k)r^2+m_alr\cos\theta$ and $M_{12}+M_{22}=I_a+m_al^2+m_alr\cos\theta$; the $J_{\mathrm{eff}}$ terms cancel, as do $B\tau$ and $D\dot q$ because $(1,1)B=(1,1)D=0$. Setting $\ddot\theta=\dot\theta=0$ and linearising $\cos\theta$ gives~\eqref{eq:kappa}.
\end{proof}
\begin{corollary}[Acceleration budget]
For this robot $\kappa=0.176$\,rad per m/s$^2$ ($10.1^\circ$ per m/s$^2$). A sustained acceleration $a$ requires a lean of $10.1^\circ a$; the off-ball limit of $30^\circ$ caps quasi-static acceleration near 3\,m/s$^2$, and a policy that leans $1^\circ$ accelerates at only $\approx$0.1\,m/s$^2$.
\end{corollary}
Identity~\eqref{eq:identity} contains no control or drive terms, so it must hold on every trajectory of every controller. It is therefore a controller-independent test of the derived inertia model.

\begin{remark}[Constant velocity]
At constant velocity $v$ the equilibrium is upright ($\theta=0$) with torque $\tau=d_{\mathrm{eff}}v/r$ to overcome wheel damping, about 23\,N\,m in the plane at 1\,m/s, which the allocation spreads over three wheels well within $\tau_{\max}$. The planar model therefore predicts no torque-imposed speed limit near 1\,m/s.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Validation against the simulator}
We test the model on the full 3-D simulation (contact-frame stepper, implicit integration). (i) The momentum identity~\eqref{eq:identity}, evaluated on trajectories of three different controllers, has a regression slope of 0.88--0.93 against its right-hand side ($R^2$ 0.60--0.82; \S\ref{sec:theory}). (ii) An LQR designed on $(A,B_s)$ alone stabilises the simulated robot (\S\ref{sec:lqr}). (iii) Instantaneous accelerations under a torque step match for the ball (3.17 vs.\ 3.01\,rad/s$^2$ per 0.5\,N\,m) but not for the body (0.73 vs.\ 1.60), because MuJoCo's soft contacts allow initial creep at the ball--floor contact (measured ball acceleration 2.5$\times$ the rolling value). (iv) Measured wheel rates differ from Eq.~\eqref{eq:W} by a constant 27--28\% across speeds, indicating an error in the effective rolling radius of the capsule contact rather than speed-dependent slip. We report these mismatches as limits of the planar model rather than tuning them away.

\section{Controllers}\label{sec:ctrl}
\subsection{Model-based: LQR with reference governor}\label{sec:lqr}
For each plane we minimise $\int (s^\top Qs+R\tau^2)\,dt$ with $Q=\mathrm{diag}(10^{-6},50,1,1)$, $R=1$, giving $\tau=-K(s-s_{\mathrm{ref}})$ with $K=(-0.001,-76.1,-4.33,-11.6)$ (negative entries because $B_s$ has opposite signs on $\varphi$ and $\theta$; magnitudes $(0,76.1,4.33,11.6)$) from the continuous algebraic Riccati equation and closed-loop poles $\{-20.3,-5.02,-4.50,\approx0\}$. Velocity tracking uses $s_{\mathrm{ref}}=(\cdot,0,v_{\mathrm{ref}}/r,0)$. Because of the non-minimum-phase zero, a step in $v_{\mathrm{ref}}$ demands an impulsive torque, so a reference governor moves $v_{\mathrm{ref}}$ toward the command at most $a_{\max}=0.5$\,m/s$^2$ (a lean of about $5^\circ$ by the corollary). A slow outer integral on the measured body velocity removes steady error from contact creep. Yaw uses $T_z=-k_\psi(\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}}-\omega_z)$. Planar torques for the $x$ and $y$ planes and $T_z$ form $\bm T$, mapped to wheels by $\bm\tau_w=W^{-\top}R^\top\bm T$ with the body rotation $R$. The controller reads body attitude and rates (IMU) and ball rotation (wheel odometry); it was not tuned on the simulator beyond choosing $a_{\max}$, $k_\psi$ and the integral gain.

\subsection{Learned: PPO velocity-tracking policies}\label{sec:rl}
\textbf{MDP.} The state observed by the policy is $o_t=(\bm\rho_t,\bm\omega_t,\bm v_t,\bm\omega_{w,t},a_{t-1},c_t,\sin\psi_t,\cos\psi_t)$: body orientation as a rotation vector, body angular and linear velocity, wheel speeds, the previous action, the command $c=(v_x,v_y,\omega_z)$ and the heading. The action $a_t\in[-1,1]^3$ is the normalised wheel-torque vector, $\bm\tau_w=\tau_{\max}a_t$. Commands are resampled every 2--3\,s: zero with probability 0.1, otherwise speed $\sim\mathcal U[0,v_{\max}]$, direction $\sim\mathcal U[0,2\pi)$, yaw rate $\sim\mathcal U[-\omega_{\max},\omega_{\max}]$. Episodes last 8\,s and terminate with a fall penalty when tilt exceeds its limit or the body--ball angle $\theta_b$ exceeds $\theta_{\max}$ (\S\ref{sec:exploit}).

\textbf{Rewards.} With $v$ the planar velocity, $u=c_{xy}/\lVert c_{xy}\rVert$, $p=v\cdot u$ and $e=\lVert v-c_{xy}\rVert$, the tracking terms compared were
\begin{align}
r_{\mathrm{dot}}&=\min(p,\lVert c_{xy}\rVert)-\tfrac12\lVert v-pu\rVert,\\
r_{\mathrm{err}}&=\lVert c_{xy}\rVert-e+\tfrac12 e^{-e^2/0.25^2},\\
r_{\mathrm{lean}}&=k_\ell\big(1-\min(p/\lVert c_{xy}\rVert,1)\big)\,\mathrm{clip}(\ell/0.3,-1,1),
\end{align}
plus a yaw analogue at weight 0.5, where $\ell$ is the body-over-ball offset along $u$ divided by its height. Regularisers: $-10^{-4}\lVert a_t\rVert^2$, an action-rate term $k_r\lVert a_t-a_{t-1}\rVert^2$, and a soft off-ball term $-k_b(\theta_b/\theta_{\max})^2$.

\textbf{Optimisation.} PPO~\cite{schulman2017} maximises the clipped surrogate
\begin{equation}
L^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\phi)=\mathbb E_t\big[\min(\rho_t\hat A_t,\ \mathrm{clip}(\rho_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t)\big],
\end{equation}
with $\rho_t=\pi_\phi(a_t|o_t)/\pi_{\phi_{\mathrm{old}}}(a_t|o_t)$, generalised advantages $\hat A_t=\sum_k(\gamma\lambda)^k\delta_{t+k}$, $\delta_t=r_t+\gamma V(o_{t+1})-V(o_t)$~\cite{schulman2016}, a squared-error value loss and an entropy bonus. We use $\epsilon=0.2$, $\gamma=0.995$, $\lambda=0.95$, target KL 0.03, learning rate $3{\times}10^{-4}\!\to\!3{\times}10^{-5}$, 7 environments $\times$ 512 steps per update, minibatch 1792, 8 epochs, and separate $2{\times}256$ tanh actor and critic MLPs (143k parameters) in Stable-Baselines3~\cite{raffin2021}, with running observation normalisation.

\textbf{Simulation throughput.} The contact-frame correction must run between collision detection and constraint construction, so the original RK4 step is executed in Python stages (four evaluations per step). Switching the integrator to MuJoCo's implicit-fast scheme (one evaluation) cut the control-step cost from 3.26 to 1.10\,ms; RK4-trained policies produced the same speed curves within 0.01\,m/s on the new integrator, so later runs use it.

\subsection{Hybrid: residual policy on the LQR}
The residual controller applies $a_t=\mathrm{clip}\big(a^{\mathrm{LQR}}_t+\beta\,\pi_\phi(o_t)\big)$ with $\beta=0.3$, so the LQR supplies a stabilising, heading-invariant prior and the policy learns corrections where the linear model fails~\cite{johannink2019,silver2018}. \tbd{results}

\section{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:eval}
All controllers are evaluated deterministically on the nominal robot with the same scripts:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Speed grid}: 8 directions $\times$ speeds 0.25--1.0\,m/s (and up to the training $v_{\max}$), 4\,s each, 2 repeats; achieved velocity averaged over the last 2\,s. \emph{Speed ratio} is achieved/commanded speed; RMSE is over commands $\le$1\,m/s; grid falls counts falls within those trials.
\item \textbf{Yaw}: spin in place at $\pm1$\,rad/s.
\item \textbf{Transients}: rise time to 90\% of 0.5\,m/s; reversal time from $+0.5$ to $-0.5$\,m/s; a 0.5\,m/s arc at 1\,rad/s.
\item \textbf{Heading}: 0.5\,m/s commands in 4 directions from 6 random start headings.
\item \textbf{Smoothness}: mean $\lVert a_t-a_{t-1}\rVert^2$ over all tests.
\item \textbf{Stability}: 30 episodes of random training-distribution commands, recording the command at each fall.
\item \textbf{Path following}: a square course (four 1.5\,m legs at 0.5\,m/s), a 1\,m/s leg, a turning leg and a diagonal leg; the planned path integrates the commands, time limits are disabled so the robot is never silently reset, and a fall ends the run.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Relaxing limits invites a ball-ejection exploit}\label{sec:exploit}
At the original actuator envelope (10\,N\,m, damping 0.8, tilt limit $20^\circ$) policies balanced but barely moved (ratio 0.06). Raising torque to 30\,N\,m, lowering damping to 0.1 and the tilt limit to $70^\circ$ increased reward, but a trace showed the policy driving the ball out from under the body: $\theta_b$ reached $59^\circ$ at 0.2\,s and $88^\circ$ at 0.6\,s while the ball rolled away at $\approx$1\,m/s and the body stood on its wheels. Body tilt never reached the limit. \finding{A tilt limit is not a ballbot fall detector; the constraint that defines the task, $\theta_b<\theta_{\max}$, must be enforced explicitly.}

\subsection{Reward shaping did not fix timid tracking}\label{sec:timid}
With $\theta_{\max}=30^\circ$ enforced, stronger rewards, lower fall penalties, smoothness penalties, the lean bonus and the error reward (S2--S5) all stayed at ratios $\le$0.36. The policies used 1--3\% of the available torque and leaned about $1^\circ$, which by the corollary yields $\approx$0.1\,m/s$^2$; yet given an out-of-distribution 2\,m/s command, an S4 policy reached 1.21\,m/s. The robot could go faster; the policies chose not to.

\subsection{The command distribution was the bottleneck}\label{sec:cmd}
Every policy trained with $\omega_{\max}=3$\,rad/s fell in 20--30 of 30 random-command episodes; for \texttt{trk\_a}, 23 of 29 falls occurred under yaw commands above 1\,rad/s (\texttt{trk\_c}: 24 of 29). Capping $\omega_{\max}=1$ and training from scratch (\texttt{trk\_f}) raised the ratio at 1\,m/s from 0.31 to 0.91 and at 0.5\,m/s to 0.98 (second seed 0.95 and 1.08). Fine-tuning the timid policies instead reached 0.59 or collapsed (\texttt{trk\_e}, 30/30 falls). \finding{For an unstable balancer, infeasible commands in the training distribution do not merely fail on their own; they suppress learning of the feasible ones, and policies that learned to avoid motion do not easily unlearn it.}

\subsection{Smoothness as a second stage}\label{sec:smooth}
Continuing \texttt{trk\_f} (\texttt{trk\_g}) cut the action rate $11\times$ (0.147 to 0.013) while keeping the ratio at 0.99 at 0.5\,m/s and improving yaw error from 0.40 to 0.12\,rad/s; a stronger action-rate penalty (\texttt{trk\_h}) gave the fewest falls (3/30). Applied from the start (S3), the same penalties froze motion. Fig.~\ref{fig:smooth} shows the trade-off.

\section{Theory Versus Learning}\label{sec:theory}
\subsection{Do learned policies obey the derived physics?}
We apply the three structural results to step, reversal and stop manoeuvres of the LQR and two PPO policies (\texttt{analyze\_theory.py}; Fig.~\ref{fig:theory}).

\textbf{Momentum identity.} Regressing the left-hand side of~\eqref{eq:identity} on $m_agl\sin\theta$ from filtered ball and body rates gives slopes of 0.93 (LQR), 0.88 (\texttt{trk\_g}) and 0.89 (\texttt{trk\_h}) against an ideal 1, with $R^2$ of 0.82, 0.60 and 0.60. Because the identity contains no control term, this validates the derived inertia matrix on trajectories of arbitrary controllers.

\textbf{Non-minimum-phase reversal.} After a 0.5\,m/s forward step, all three controllers first move the ball backward: by 1.7\,cm (LQR), 0.3\,cm (\texttt{trk\_g}) and 1.1\,cm (\texttt{trk\_h}), within the first 0.3\,s, as Proposition~\ref{prop:nmp} requires.

\textbf{Lean--acceleration law.} On quasi-static samples ($|\ddot\theta|<0.5$\,rad/s$^2$) the lean regresses on acceleration with slopes of 0.168 (LQR, 95\% of $\kappa$, $R^2=0.86$), 0.160 (\texttt{trk\_g}, 91\%, $R^2=0.73$) and 0.146 (\texttt{trk\_h}, 83\%, $R^2=0.67$). \finding{The learned policies, trained from reward alone, rediscovered the lean law implied by the Lagrangian.} They differ in \emph{how} they use it: PPO leans faster but less deeply (peak $\approx 6^\circ$ at 0.5\,s versus the LQR's $9^\circ$ at 0.8\,s), trading peak acceleration for a smoother approach.

\subsection{Head-to-head: derived versus learned control}
Table~\ref{tab:tracking} (first row) and Fig.~\ref{fig:speed} compare the LQR with the learned policies. Inside the linear model's validity, the LQR is better on almost every axis: speed ratio 1.00--1.08 at 0.25--0.75\,m/s, rise time 0.89\,s, heading error 0.02\,m/s with no falls (it is heading-invariant by construction, whereas the best policies show 0.31\,m/s error and 21--38\% falls from random headings), and an action rate of 0.0002, 50$\times$ smoother than the smoothest policy. Outside it, the learned policy wins: the LQR falls in every 1\,m/s trial and on the square course's turning leg, while \texttt{trk\_h} completes the full course including 1\,m/s and 0.5\,m/s while turning at 1\,rad/s. Neither yaw controller is strong ($\pm0.57$\,rad/s for the LQR at $\pm1$ commanded), which we attribute to the drive: equal wheel torques mostly spin the ball against negligible floor torsional friction.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\columnwidth]{figures/fig_speed_tracking.pdf}
\caption{Achieved versus commanded speed (mean over 8 directions; dashed: ideal).}

\textbf{The 1\,m/s edge.} Both controller families fail near 1\,m/s, which suggests a physical cause. Diagnostics on the LQR rule out the obvious ones: commanded torque stays below 17\% of the limit until the final 0.2\,s, wheel normal forces are steady at 17--18\,N, and the wheel-rate discrepancy is constant across speed (no growing slip). The failure coincides with an unmodelled yaw spin-up (to 0.9\,rad/s) and a growing body--ball oscillation, consistent with 3-D coupling absent from the planar model. We tested one candidate, gyroscopic torques of the rotors ($J_w\omega_w\approx0.2$\,N\,m\,s per wheel at 40\,rad/s), by re-deriving the model and a discrete-time LQR (designed at the 100\,Hz control period; a continuous design becomes sampled-data unstable when light rotors push its fastest pole to $-135\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$) for rotor inertias of 0.0005, 0.002 and 0.005\,kg\,m$^2$ and simulating each. All three track 0.75\,m/s without falls and fall in every trial from 1\,m/s, reaching 0.84, 0.92--0.95 and 0.99--1.01\,m/s respectively before falling. Lighter rotors make the edge slightly \emph{earlier}, so rotor gyroscopics are not the cause. The remaining candidates are the omniwheel contact model at high sliding speed (the source of the unexplained yaw torque) and ball--floor contact effects; resolving this is the most important open modelling question.

\subsection{Path following}
Fig.~\ref{fig:paths} shows the square course. Velocity tracking does not correct position, so error accumulates (0.35--0.58\,m mean distance from the plan). A naive position-feedback outer loop did not help consistently because it pushes commanded speeds toward the stability edge. After a 4\,rad turn, the learned policies execute world-frame commands in the wrong direction; training always started at the same heading, so we now randomise it.

\subsection{Spin-range curriculum}
Continuing the S6/S7 policies with $\omega_{\max}=1.5$\,rad/s (S8) improved yaw tracking at $\pm1$\,rad/s (e.g.\ \texttt{spin\_c}: $-0.95/+0.82$ versus $-0.49/+0.51$) but lowered the speed ratio at 1\,m/s (0.70--0.87 versus 0.81--0.95) and left the heading error unchanged (0.31--0.34\,m/s). Widening the spin range trades translation for yaw, consistent with \S\ref{sec:cmd}.

\subsection{Robustness: pushes and parameter uncertainty}
Table~\ref{tab:push} reports push recovery at 0.5\,m/s. On the nominal robot, the learned policies are far more push-robust than the LQR: the LQR falls in 6/10 trials at 40\,N and 9/10 at 50\,N (a 50\,N, 50\,ms push is a $\approx$0.6\,m/s velocity disturbance), \texttt{trk\_g} in 1/10 and 5/10, \texttt{trk\_h} in none. Under randomised physical parameters, the picture reverses: the policies trained on the nominal model fall in 60--80\% of runs \emph{without any push}, while the LQR falls in 40\%. A single-parameter ablation on \texttt{trk\_g} isolates the cause: randomising wheel damping alone (0.05--0.3 against a nominal 0.1\,N\,m\,s) produces 5/10 falls, mass 1/10, and friction, torque scale, latency and sensor noise none. In the model, $d_{\mathrm{eff}}=d_w\lVert\bm n\rVert^2$ enters the coupling between ball and body directly (Eq.~\eqref{eq:eom}), and it is the parameter hardest to know on hardware (gearbox and motor friction). Fine-tuning \texttt{trk\_g} with randomisation and pushes (\texttt{rob\_a}) removes the brittleness (0/10 falls up to 30\,N under randomised physics, 0/10 up to 50\,N nominal) but makes the policy slow again (ratio 0.35 at 1\,m/s); stronger randomisation and 10--35\,N training pushes (\texttt{rob\_b}) give the most robust policy (at most 4/10 falls even at 50\,N under randomised physics) at a ratio of 0.27. The timid optimum of \S\ref{sec:timid} reappears when a tracking policy is fine-tuned on a harder task. Following Finding~3, we therefore retrain from scratch under randomisation, with the full and with a narrowed damping range (0.07--0.15\,N\,m\,s, the tolerance a simple bench measurement of the drivetrain would give). Trained from scratch for 20M steps, both policies are robust (0/10 falls at every nominal push up to 50\,N; \texttt{robs\_a} at most 2/10 at 50\,N under randomised physics; 3--5/30 random-command falls) and roughly twice as fast as their fine-tuned counterparts, but their achieved speed is a constant fraction of the command at every speed: 0.35 for \texttt{robs\_a} and 0.50 for \texttt{robs\_b}. A constant ratio is a steady-state gain below one, not a speed limit.

\subsection{Cascade: an outer velocity integral around the learned policy}
A steady-state gain below one is exactly what integral action removes in a classical cascade, and it is how our LQR removes contact creep. We wrap the learned policy in an outer loop that feeds it the corrected command $c'_{xy}=c_{xy}+k_i\int(c_{xy}-v)\,dt$, saturated at the policy's trained range ($\lVert c'\rVert\le1.5$\,m/s) with the integral frozen while saturated (anti-windup). With $k_i=1$, \texttt{robs\_b} tracks 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75\,m/s at 0.25, 0.51 and 0.73\,m/s with no falls and 0.79\,m/s at a 1\,m/s command (12\% falls), its heading error drops from 0.27 to 0.06\,m/s with no falls (the integral also corrects direction), it recovers from every nominal push up to 50\,N, and it remains smooth (action rate 0.003). Under randomised physics with a damping range wider than it was trained on it falls in 2--6 of 10 push trials. With $k_i=2$, the fully randomised \texttt{robs\_a} reaches 0.51\,m/s at 0.5\,m/s and saturates near 0.57\,m/s, with heading error 0.05\,m/s and the best randomised-physics push tolerance among fast controllers. \finding{Finding 5: a robust but timid learned policy plus a classical outer integral yields a controller that is robust, accurate and heading-invariant in the 0--0.75\,m/s range, combining the strengths of both families.} Giving the policy the integrated error as an observation (\texttt{robs\_c}, two seeds) helps only modestly on its own (ratio 0.54--0.62, heading error 0.19--0.23\,m/s): under randomisation, undershooting remains the policy's preferred optimum. Combined with the outer integral, however, \texttt{robs\_c} is the most accurate robust controller: 0.25, 0.51 and 0.76\,m/s at 0.25, 0.5 and 0.75\,m/s with no falls and a heading error of 0.045\,m/s.

\subsection{Rough ground: Perlin-noise floors}
We evaluate the best controllers, none of which was trained on terrain, on Perlin-noise floors of 4 and 8\,cm amplitude. At 0.5\,m/s every controller except the nominal-trained PPO policy (\texttt{trk\_h}, which slows to 0.32--0.44\,m/s with 33--38\% heading-test falls) tracks within 0.02\,m/s. At 0.75\,m/s they separate: the residual policy \texttt{res\_c} has no falls on either floor (0.81--0.82\,m/s), the LQR falls in 12--25\% of trials, and the cascaded robust policies in 12--75\%. The residual design, whose LQR prior is heading-invariant and whose learned yaw correction never saw terrain, generalises best to rough ground. Continuing training on random Perlin floors of 0--10\,cm did not help: the terrain-trained residual (\texttt{res\_t}) matches \texttt{res\_c} (no falls up to 12\,cm at $\le$0.5\,m/s, 17\% heading-test falls at 12\,cm), and the terrain-trained cascaded policy degrades with amplitude (62--88\% falls at 12\,cm).

\subsection{Step edges: toward climbing onto a book}
We drive each controller straight at a raised floor (a clean step with a 2\,cm bevel at $y=1$\,m) from a random start heading, and count a success only if the robot crosses, stops on top and holds its balance for 2\,s (10 trials per condition; Fig.~\ref{fig:step}). None of the controllers was trained on steps, yet all clear 1\,cm. At 0.7\,m/s the cascaded \texttt{robs\_c} climbs 1.5 and 2\,cm in 10/10 trials and 3\,cm in 3/10; the LQR climbs 2\,cm in 10/10 and never 3\,cm; the residual \texttt{res\_c} degrades from 7/10 at 1.5\,cm to 4/10 at 2\,cm; the nominal-trained \texttt{trk\_h} fails half the trials even on flat ground because of its heading weakness. At 0.4\,m/s no controller climbs 2\,cm. A speed sweep with the LQR reveals a \emph{climb window}: the minimum approach speed rises with step height (about 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 and 0.5--0.6\,m/s for 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5\,cm), and above $\approx$0.8\,m/s climbing fails again as the 1\,m/s instability combines with the impact at the edge. The minimum speed lies below the ballistic bound $\sqrt{2gh}$ because the motors add energy during the climb. A 2--3\,cm step, the thickness of a paperback, is therefore within reach of flat-ground controllers if they approach in the window; training for steps explicitly (\texttt{climb\_a}, \texttt{climb\_b}: 0--4\,cm steps, commands toward the step) did not extend this: at 0.7\,m/s \texttt{climb\_a} clears 2\,cm in 8--9/10 and 3\,cm in 2/10 trials, no better than the flat-trained \texttt{robs\_c}, and \texttt{climb\_b} is worse. The limit near 3\,cm is therefore physical rather than learned. A static estimate points at traction: the ball's weight share is about 40\,N, and climbing a step of height $h$ requires a horizontal force $F\approx W\tan\alpha$ with $\cos\alpha=(r-h)/r$, i.e.\ 44\,N at 3\,cm and 60\,N at 4\,cm, comparable to the total wheel--ball friction available (three wheels carrying 17--18\,N each with friction coefficient 1). Scaling the wheel--ball friction supports this: at 0.7\,m/s the \texttt{robs\_c} cascade climbs 3\,cm in 1/4 trials at nominal friction and in 4/4 at $2\times$ and $4\times$, and 4\,cm in 1/4 at $2\times$ (the LQR needs $4\times$ for any 3\,cm success at 0.5\,m/s). Design implication: a taller step needs a larger ball relative to the step ($h/r$ is 0.33 at 3\,cm), more wheel preload, or higher-friction wheels. This contrasts with our earlier book-climbing study at the original actuator envelope, where no policy crossed 1\,cm.

\subsection{Residual learning on the LQR}
A first residual attempt ($\beta=0.3$, random initial policy, commands up to 1.5\,m/s) was worse than the LQR alone (25--50\% falls at 0.5\,m/s): the untrained residual destabilised the LQR (initial episodes of $\approx$1\,s against $\approx$7\,s for the LQR alone) and the command range included speeds the LQR cannot survive. The corrected design initialises the policy's output layer to zero (the initial controller \emph{is} the LQR), uses $\beta=0.1$ with small exploration noise, and limits commands to 1\,m/s. The resulting policy (\texttt{res\_c}) keeps every strength of the LQR (heading error 0.02\,m/s with no falls, action rate 0.0002, rise time 0.90\,s, speed ratio 1.00--1.02 at 0.25--0.5\,m/s) and repairs its weakest channel: yaw tracking at $\pm1$\,rad/s improves from $\pm0.57$ to $-1.01/+0.93$, the best of any controller, and random-command falls drop to 3/30. It does not, however, extend the LQR's stability envelope: it falls in 19\% of 0.75\,m/s and 94\% of 1\,m/s trials, and its push tolerance is lower than the LQR's (6/10 falls at 30\,N). With $\beta=0.1$ the residual has enough authority to shape the yaw channel, where the LQR is merely weak, but not to rescue the balance channel near the stability edge, where the LQR is wrong. Continuing it with $\beta=0.2$ under randomisation and pushes (\texttt{res\_d}) made it worse: it overshoots (0.66\,m/s at a 0.5\,m/s command), falls in 31\% of 0.5\,m/s trials and 15/30 random-command episodes, and its push tolerance drops further (8/10 falls at 30\,N), although its heading error stays low (0.13\,m/s). Larger residual authority lets the policy fight the LQR rather than complement it.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{What the theory buys.} The derivation explains every qualitative failure we saw in learning: timid policies are timid because a $1^\circ$ lean buys only 0.1\,m/s$^2$ (corollary); fast responses are bounded by the zero at $5.8\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}$; and the drive inertia $J_{\mathrm{eff}}$, invisible in a naive pendulum-on-a-ball picture, dominates the rotational dynamics. It also yields a controller that is exact where the model is.

\textbf{What learning buys.} PPO found the same lean law without a model and remained stable in regimes the linear model does not cover. Its weaknesses (jitter, heading generalisation, sensitivity to the command distribution) are exactly where the model-based controller is strong, which is the case for residual learning.

\textbf{What the GPU study adds.} Model-based structure beats pure learning on accuracy and on the goal task; no training recipe improves robustness to pushes, pulls and rough terrain, and wider training randomisation does not help, because the falls are mechanical (the body derails off the ball); a passive ball retainer that leaves the LQR model unchanged improves every robustness measure, at a cost in tracking when trained with it. Smoothness at the command level has to be trained with, not bolted on: a motion-reference layer added to a finished policy made goal arrival worse, while the same layer used in training, with an authority that rises only when the robot manoeuvres, gave the best controller of the study (\S\ref{sec:motion}). What it cannot absorb is physical: a light ball, a heavy body, weak motors, and sustained pulls above about 3\,N. A practical lesson is that per-world masses in a batched model require recomputing the derived mass properties.

\textbf{Limitations.} Simulation only, with an idealised anisotropic omniwheel friction model and a float32 GPU engine that is not bit-reproducible; the planar model has documented mismatches (soft-contact creep, a 28\,\% transmission-rate discrepancy). The CPU-study configurations mostly use one seed (the key S6 result is replicated) and two repeats per condition. In the GPU study the clean flat benchmark uses one nominal design and 64 worlds per row, the tile evaluations 16 worlds per design and terrain kind, and most rows are single-seed; most evaluations use the training designs, and two held-out designs and a held-out terrain layout were tested for a few controllers only; there is no CPU re-evaluation of the tile policies; floor materials are plausible, not measured; and the depth sensor assumes a yaw aligned with the command. The perception test is one seed per arm. Yaw control is weak for every controller and the CPU training budgets of $\sim10^7$ steps were far below GPU-parallel locomotion studies~\cite{rudin2022}.

\textbf{Next steps.} (i) A second seed of the perception arm and the privileged height scan. (ii) Held-out robot designs, and a CPU re-evaluation of the tile policies. (iii) A structure that rewards foresight (approach-speed shaping, an imitation prior, a planner) before drawing conclusions about perception. (iv) A 3-D linearisation to explain the 1\,m/s edge. (v) Motor and compute sizing, and a sim-to-real study; no hardware result is claimed here.
