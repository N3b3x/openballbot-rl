Rules and bounds

What a ballbot can and cannot do

Ten limits the study measured, written as rules for a robot builder. Each rule leads with the number. The reason, the tables and the advice open on click.

Every number is cited to a log entry (S-number) in docs/research/tracking_study_log.md. Computed values say so. Many rules were measured on earlier checkpoints, which are named; the recommended controller today is g39_goalcentre_s0 (S121).

The ten rules

  1. Traction. From rest, a ball cannot climb a step above about \(0.29\,r\): 2.6 cm here. Rule 1
  2. Derailing. Without arrestors the body slides off the ball at about 15 degrees. Rule 2
  3. Authority. A dial between speed tracking and push robustness; 0.25 is the knee. Rule 3
  4. LQR envelope. The gains are only as good as the mass model. Rule 4
  5. Motor headroom. A tenth of the torque is a wall for every controller. Rule 5
  6. Froude scaling. Speeds scale with \(\sqrt{s}\), torques with \(s^4\). Rule 6
  7. Planner margin. It must leave the narrowest gap open. Rule 7
  8. Sensor aim. Stabilised head, 25 degrees down, aimed at the plan. Rule 8
  9. Obstacle size. Size decides; slowing down buys 0.03 to 0.05. Rule 9
  10. Pushes near obstacles. A balance limit, not a planning limit. Rule 10

Rule 1
Terrain

The traction limit

From rest, a ball of radius \(r\) cannot roll up a step higher than about \(0.29\,r\): 2.6 cm on the 9 cm ball.

Measured: 2.9 cm

The 9 cm ball passes half of the steps at 2.9 cm. Prediction: 2.6 cm.

S125
A run-up adds about 1 cm

At 0.6 m/s the limit moves from about 2.8 to about 4 cm. At 0.9 m/s the robot falls on flat ground.

S126
For a robot

Choose the ball first: radius about 3.4 times the tallest step.

S88, S95
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. A driven ball of radius \(r\) on a floor with friction coefficient \(\mu\) cannot roll up a step higher than \(h_{\max}=r\,(1-\cos(\arctan\mu))\), which is about \(0.29\,r\) when \(\mu\approx1\).

Why

To climb a step the ballbot's ball must lift off the floor and pivot on the step edge. The contact force at the edge points from the edge to the ball centre, and the steeper that direction, the more sideways (tangential) force the ball needs relative to the normal force.

Friction supplies at most \(\mu\) times the normal force. Once the edge contact is steeper than \(\arctan\mu\), the ball slips instead of climbing, whatever the motors do. This is the traction limit (S88).

$$\cos\theta = 1-\frac{h}{r},\qquad \tan\theta < \mu \;\Rightarrow\; h < h_{\max}=r\bigl(1-\cos(\arctan\mu)\bigr)$$

Quasi-static and simplified: no running start, no body lean. A 0.6 m/s run-up lifts the measured limit of the 9 cm ball from about 2.8 to about 4 cm (S126; g39_goalcentre, three seeds, 128 worlds per tile kind and seed, DR 1, noise on). With \(\mu=1\), \(\theta=45^\circ\) and \(h_{\max}=0.29\,r\).

Evidence

The then-recommended g18_ret_rs025_s1, trained only on 9 cm-ball designs, was run at 0.3 m/s over up-steps and bumps for 20 s on the nominal robot and on a never-trained 1.5x robot with a 13.5 cm ball (S88, 640 worlds per kind).

For a real robot

Small robot. The climbable step shrinks with the ball: the 6.75 cm ball of the 0.75x design has a limit of 1.95 cm (S93). A floor friction of 0.4, the low end of the simulated floor classes (S88), gives \(h_{\max}=0.07\,r\) from the equation, about 6 mm on a 9 cm ball (computed, not measured).

Large robot. The 18 cm ball of the 2x design has a limit of 5.2 cm (S93). Ball radius and ball-floor friction set the climbable height; the controller does not. If the robot must cross a 4 cm sill, choose the ball first.

Design for your ballbot. Choose the ball radius for the tallest step the robot must climb: about 3.4 times the step height when ball-floor friction is near 1 (the traction limit 0.29 r), and about 4.2 times when the friction is unknown (the measured 50 percent fall point sits at 0.17 to 0.29 r, mean 0.24 r, over floor friction 0.4 to 1.2, which is the traction limit averaged over that friction spread). Keep ball-floor friction high. Then add passive arrestors for the falls that are not traction (Rule 2), and give the motors torque headroom (Rule 5).

The scale study confirmed the rule: one recipe trained on four robot sizes, two training seeds (trained at free-rolling friction 0.001, evaluated on the corrected contact model at 0.01, S98 and S99). The 2.3 to 5.9 cm step band is fatal for the 6.75 cm ball (1.00 on both seeds), nearly fatal for the 9 cm ball (0.93 to 0.95) and survivable for the 13.5 and 18 cm balls (0.56 to 0.65 and 0.56 to 0.60). A 4.5 cm-ball robot needs its own training: the shared policy did not transfer that far down and failed almost everywhere, flat ground included (0.90, S95).

Rule 2
Mechanism

Derailing, and why arrestors fix it

Without arrestors the body slides off the ball at about 15 degrees of tilt. Three passive rollers stop it.

The cause

0.95 of undisturbed falls ended with the body leaving the ball. The wheels were at their torque limit 0.7 percent of the time.

S79
The fix

Arrestors at 100 degrees: goal success 0.57 to 0.75 with no retraining.

S79
For a robot

Fit arrestors before tuning the controller. Place them at the loaded pose.

S79
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. A ballbot held on its ball only by gravity and three wheel contacts falls by sliding off the ball at about 15 degrees of tilt; three passive arrestors placed just below the ball's equator at the loaded seat remove that failure.

Why

The body sits on the ball; nothing holds it there except its own weight pressing the wheels down. When the body tilts, the wheel contacts shift and, past a point, the body and ball axes separate. We call this derailing (S79).

An arrestor is a small passive roller that just touches the ball when the robot stands still. It carries no force while seated and only acts when the body starts to lift or slide. Any contact force on a sphere passes through its centre, so the arrestors add no torque about the ball and leave the planar model, and the LQR built on it, untouched.

Mounts between 90 and 100 degrees from the top of the ball are equal within noise; 100 degrees was chosen because only a mount below the equator resists the body lifting off. A mount diametrically opposite the wheel (135 degrees) sits 1.2 cm above the floor and would touch it at about 21 degrees of tilt, so it is not buildable on this ball (S79).

Evidence

A per-step fall diagnosis over 1760 worlds (S79): in 0.95 of undisturbed falls the episode ended by the body-ball offset limit with the body still below 70 degrees, 0.78 of them derailed before 25 degrees, the median tilt at derailing was 14.7 degrees, and the wheels were at their torque limit in 0.7 percent of the last second. Pulls were active in 58 percent of disturbed falls, pushes in 3 percent.

For a real robot

Any size. Fit arrestors before tuning the controller: this was the only change in the study that moved disturbance robustness. Place them at the pose the robot settles into under its own weight, not at the CAD rest pose (see lesson 6).

Small robot. Floor clearance is the constraint. At 100 degrees the arrestor bottom is 6.5 cm above the floor on the 9 cm ball and touches only beyond 57 degrees of tilt; lower mounts lose clearance fast (3.3 cm at 120 degrees). Clearance scales with the ball radius, so a smaller ball forbids low mounts sooner.

Large robot. Real ball-transfer units weigh about 10 g (S79); on a large robot the arrestor's mass and friction are negligible against the body.

Rule 3
Controller

The authority dial

Residual authority is a dial between exact speed tracking and push robustness. 0.25 is the knee.

Today the dial moves

The recommended controller g39_goalcentre_s0 uses 0.15 while cruising and 0.3 when manoeuvring.

S112, S121
For a robot

Hold a commanded speed: stay near 0.25. Only survive shoves: up to 0.5, and pay 10 to 30 percent of the speed.

S82, S83
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. How much the learned residual may override the LQR is a dial between speed-tracking accuracy and push robustness; with arrestors, authority 0.25 keeps the LQR's tracking exactly and gains most of the robustness.

Why

The applied action is the LQR command plus a scaled residual from the PPO policy. The scale is the residual authority \(\alpha\):

$$a=\operatorname{clip}\bigl(a_{\mathrm{LQR}}+\alpha\,a_{\pi},\,-1,\,1\bigr)$$

The LQR is what tracks a commanded speed precisely. The residual learns what the LQR cannot: recovering from pushes, pulls and terrain. The more it may act, the more it also disturbs the LQR's tracking; at \(\alpha=1\) the sum is clipped to the same range as the policy's own exploration noise, so the residual can cancel the stabilising action outright. Authorities above 0.5 were not measured on the corrected engine, so the table below is the whole evidence.

For a real robot

Pick the authority for the job. A robot that must hold a commanded speed (following a person, docking) wants 0.25. A robot that mostly has to survive shoves and does not care about exact speed can go to 0.5 or to pure learning, and pays 10 to 30 percent of its commanded speed for a further 0.05 to 0.08 of push success.

Stay at or below 0.5. That is the range measured on the corrected engine; above it the structure stops being an LQR with a correction and becomes pure learning with an offset, which is the row that tracks worst.

Unknown parameters. The LQR base is also what carries the controller to a robot it never saw (Rule 5 and S85-R), which argues for keeping the authority low on real hardware whose parameters are only approximately known.

S98
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. The planar-model LQR with per-design gains is only as good as the modelled ball-to-body mass ratio: with a quarter-mass ball the body unseats within the first second, and with four times the base mass it falls in about a fifth of driving worlds; the earlier 20 s standing comparison of Q, R and the integral term was a simulator artefact and is void (S98).

Why

The LQR gains come from the planar model: one vertical plane, ball and body as two rigid bodies coupled through rolling and the motor torque. The gains minimise the quadratic cost with the Q and R weights below, and a velocity integral term removes slow drift.

$$J=\int_0^\infty \bigl(s^\top Q\,s+R\,\tau^2\bigr)\,dt,\qquad Q=\mathrm{diag}(10^{-6},\,50,\,1,\,1),\; R=1,\; k_i=1$$

State \(s=(\varphi,\theta,\dot\varphi,\dot\theta)\): ball angle, body tilt and their rates. The derivation is on the math page.

When the ball is very light compared with the body, the real contact dynamics stop matching the model; an earlier analytic check in the log (design-space section) found the planar model least faithful when the body is more than about 60 times heavier than the ball. The 0.25x design puts a 42 g ball under a 3.9 kg body (S93), a ratio of about 93 (computed from those two masses).

Evidence

Void: the 20 s standing comparison (S93, S94, S96, S97). An LQR gain sweep standing still for 20 s appeared to show that no choice of R or integral gain rescues the quarter-mass or four-times ball and that the integral term is essential. S98 found that most of those "falls" were the simulator's state going non-finite in one 10 ms step while the robot stood upright at 1 to 2 degrees of tilt: the wheel-ball contacts use a 1000 to 1 anisotropic friction ratio, which the float32 contact solver cannot hold at a near-stationary contact, preferentially on heavy-ball designs. The table that stood here has been removed; see lesson 8 and the non-finite termination entry.

For a real robot

Keep the ball mass near what the model says. If the real ball is much lighter or heavier than the modelled one (a hollow shell, a filled bowling ball), re-derive the gains from measured masses before blaming the controller.

Keep the velocity integral as designed. In the model it removes steady drift. The simulated evidence that it is essential for a 20 s hold was part of the void comparison and awaits re-measurement, so treat its value as a modelling argument for now, not a measured one.

Small robot. A light robot naturally has a light ball and a high body-to-ball ratio; check the ratio stays well below the 0.25x design's 93 before trusting the planar gains. The light-ball failure is real: it unseats, it does not blow up (S98).

Large robot. Scaled designs (0.75x, 1.5x, 2x) with masses scaled by \(s^3\) keep the nominal ball-to-body ratio, so the planar gains carry over; their standing numbers are not stated here until the fixed contact model has been re-run on them (S98).

S85-R
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. A design with a tenth of the nominal wheel torque reaches the goal at most 0.30 of the time under every controller, and adding it to training does not help: the limit is the motor, not the policy.

Why

Balancing an inverted pendulum means producing enough torque fast enough to catch a growing lean. The torque needed to hold a lean grows with body weight times the height of its centre of mass, \(m_a g l\); the earlier analytic sweep in the log found the LQR-recoverable tilt drops below 20 degrees when the torque authority \(\tau_{\max}/(m_a g l)\) falls to about 6.9, a threshold the sweep states with 6 N m per wheel for the nominal robot (design-space section of the log). In the simulator the nominal torque limit is 30 per wheel (configs/env/dz_nom.yaml, in its actuator units; Table C of the setup page), and 6 is the limit of the weak-motor design tau6. The analytic sweep and the simulator use different torque scales that the log does not reconcile, so the 6.9 threshold should not be compared directly with the simulator designs.

$$\text{torque authority}=\frac{\tau_{\max}}{m_a\,g\,l}$$

With a tenth of the torque the recoverable lean is tiny, and no policy, model-based or learned, can command torque that is not there.

For a real robot

Size the motors from the lean, not the speed. Torque is a hard ceiling: check \(\tau_{\max}\) against \(m_a g l\) before anything else. The design-space page's rules give the authority gauge per design.

Large robot. For geometric similarity the torque requirement scales with \(s^4\) (Rule 6): a 2x robot needs 16 times the wheel torque, computed from the scaling law. Motors are usually the first thing that stops a ballbot from scaling up.

Small robot. The same law gives a 0.75x robot about a third of the nominal torque requirement; small ballbots are rarely torque limited but are more sensitive to latency, because they fall faster (Rule 6).

Rule 6
Scaling

Froude scaling

Scale a ballbot by \(s\): speeds scale with \(\sqrt{s}\), masses with \(s^3\), wheel torque with \(s^4\), the fall rate with \(1/\sqrt{s}\).

Convert every speed

0.3 m/s on the 9 cm ball is about 0.37 m/s on a 1.5x robot and 0.26 m/s on a 0.75x robot (computed).

scaling law
Small falls faster

The same latency is a larger share of the fall time.

Large needs torque

scaling law
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. Scaling a ballbot by a geometric factor \(s\) scales its natural speeds by \(\sqrt{s}\), its masses by \(s^3\), its wheel torque by \(s^4\) and its fall rate (the unstable pole) by \(1/\sqrt{s}\); commands, pushes and evaluation speeds in this study are scaled the same way.

Why

Froude scaling keeps the ratio of inertial to gravitational effects fixed. Gravity does not scale, so a robot that is \(s\) times longer has pendulum dynamics \(\sqrt{s}\) times slower, and the speed at which it behaves "the same" is \(\sqrt{s}\) times higher.

$$v\propto\sqrt{s},\qquad m\propto s^{3},\qquad \tau\propto s^{4},\qquad p\propto s^{-1/2}$$

\(p\) is the unstable pole of the linearised model; \(\tau\propto s^4\) follows from \(m_a g l\propto s^3\cdot s\). Damping is scaled with \(s^{2.5}\) and rotor inertia with \(s^5\) in scripts/study/design_space.py.

The lean law, how many degrees the body leans per m/s\(^2\) of acceleration, is nearly scale independent, so a scaled robot looks the same in slow motion.

Evidence

The log's design-space analysis gives \(p\propto s^{-1/2}\): a half-size robot must be controlled \(\sqrt2\) times faster. The command range in every design's environment file is \(1.2\sqrt{s}\) m/s and pushes scale with the robot's weight (design_space.py).

On the corrected engine: the LQR alone is re-derived per scale from the same planar model for the 0.75x, 1.5x and 2x robots (S93; their standing numbers are not stated until the fixed contact model of S98 has been re-run on them); g18_ret_rs025_s1, trained only at scale 1, reaches 0.71 of goals on the never-seen 1.5x robot and 0.66 with pushes and pulls (S85-R). The scale study on crossing success per scale is in progress (S93). S85-R, S93, log design-space section

For a real robot

Convert every speed. A result quoted at 0.3 m/s for the 9 cm-ball robot corresponds to about 0.37 m/s on a 1.5x robot and 0.26 m/s on a 0.75x robot (computed from \(\sqrt{s}\)).

Small robot. Faster dynamics mean the same control latency is a larger fraction of the fall time; budget sensor and loop delay in Froude-scaled time, not milliseconds.

Large robot. Torque grows as \(s^4\) while mass grows as \(s^3\): motors and gearboxes, not balance, set the practical upper size.

Rule 7
Navigation

Planner margin against gap width

The planner margin must leave the narrowest gap open. On 0.7 m gaps, 0.22 m works and 0.30 m gets most robots stuck.

Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. The obstacle inflation margin must leave the narrowest gap in the environment open on the planner grid; on 0.7 m gaps with 5 cm cells, 0.22 m works and 0.30 m or more turns a solved course into one where most robots get stuck.

Why

A wavefront planner treats every cell within the margin of an obstacle as blocked. Two blocks a distance \(g\) apart leave a corridor of \(g-2m\), and on a grid of cell size \(c\) that corridor must still contain cells the robot can pass through.

$$g-2m \;\gtrsim\; \text{a few cells}\qquad (g=0.7\ \mathrm{m},\ c=0.05\ \mathrm{m}:\ m=0.22 \Rightarrow 0.26\ \mathrm{m},\ m=0.30 \Rightarrow 0.10\ \mathrm{m})$$

The corridor widths are computed from the margins the log tested; the pass/stuck outcome is measured.

A margin that looks safer on paper therefore fails in a different way: instead of brushing blocks the robot sees no route and stands still, which the protocol counts as failure.

For a real robot

Measure the narrowest doorway first. Set the margin from \((g_{\min}-\text{robot width})/2\) minus a cell or two, not from how much clearance feels comfortable.

Small robot. A smaller footprint allows a smaller margin for the same gaps, but the grid cell should shrink with it or the corridor rounds to zero cells.

Large robot. A 2x robot with a 2x footprint needs 2x gaps for the same margin ratio; doors that the 9 cm-ball robot passes with 0.22 m to spare become impassable.

Do not use the margin to fix pushes. See Rule 10.

S90
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. Mount the range sensor on a stabilised head, pitch it about 25 degrees down, and let the navigation layer aim it at the planner's target heading; where the sensor looks matters more than how many pixels it has.

Why

The planner only knows about obstacles the sensor has seen. A time-of-flight sensor rigid with a leaning body sweeps up and down with every correction; one aimed too steeply sees mostly the near ground and blocks enter view late, so the memory occupancy map stays incomplete and the robot gets stuck or rolls onto a block.

With a smooth, rate-limited path follower the commanded heading turns slowly, so a sensor yawed to the command direction looks at where the robot is going now, not where the planner is about to send it. Aiming it at the planner's target heading brings side blocks into view in time (S90).

For a real robot

Buy a gimbal before buying pixels. An 18 x 14 zone ToF on a stabilised head gets within 6 points of a perfect map; a denser depth camera at the same mount is no better, and the best camera on the body cones is the worst configuration tested.

Let the planner own the aim. On hardware the navigation layer commands the gimbal yaw to its target heading; in simulation this is the yaw_override hook (S90).

Small robot. A low mount (base origin) loses 7 points; the head mount here is 15 cm above the base origin, so on a small robot find the height that still sees over the ball.

Large robot. Pitch is set by the distance at which blocks must enter view; a taller head needs a shallower pitch than -25 degrees to see the same ground distance.

S89
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. Obstacle size sets what is crossable (traction, Rule 1); slowing down before a feature buys little (0.03 to 0.05 of falls, mostly stairs, S103); route around what cannot be crossed and size the ball for the steps you need.

Why

Rule 1 says a step above \(0.29\,r\) cannot be climbed, however slowly it is approached. Below that height the controller can manage, and there the approach matters: arriving slowly gives the balance loop time to absorb the jolt. An earlier conclusion that speed made no difference rested on two artefacts in turn: an 8 s test in which slow robots never reached the feature (the exposure artefact, S86, lesson 3), and then the solver blow-up of S98, which inflated the falls of a creeping, nearly stationary robot most of all (lesson 8).

Foresight given to the balance policy alone did not help: a perfect height scan or a depth sensor as policy input left the obstacle falls where they were (S84, S88). Using the map to slow down before the feature buys little once the slowed robot is given time to reach it (S103): a 15 s test had shown a halving, but slowed robots there covered only 0.96 to 1.07 m against features from 0.6 m out, the exposure artefact again.

For a real robot

Route around what you cannot cross. Above the traction limit no speed helps, so choose the ball first (Rule 1) and plan around (Rule 8). A slow-down layer near features is worth only 0.03 to 0.05 of falls and is not worth a hardware sensor on its own (S103).

Terrain sensing is for routing. A perfect map turned 0.91 falls into 0.09 where obstacles could be avoided (S89). Feeding it to the balance policy alone buys nothing (S84, S88).

Small robot. Every threshold and cable cover is a wall above its traction limit.

Large robot. The 13.5 cm ball already crosses what the 9 cm ball cannot (Rule 1); the by-size thresholds scale with \(r\).

S121
Why, the evidence tables, and what it means for a robot

Full statement. A push beside an unclimbable block can carry the robot into it before any planner reacts; this is a balance-recovery limit, and neither a wider planner margin nor training the policy with the planner in the loop reduces it.

Why

The planner replans every 0.2 s and keeps a 0.22 m margin from blocks. A 5 to 20 N push for 50 ms, or a 3 to 12 N pull for up to 2 s, moves the robot further than that margin before the recovery finishes, and once a wheel rolls onto an 8 cm block (3 times the traction limit) the fall is decided.

A wider margin cannot help because it closes the gaps (Rule 7). Teaching the balance policy about blocks cannot help because the frozen policy already follows the planner's smooth commands; what it loses is the push recovery itself.

For a real robot

Budget for it. In cluttered spaces with people, expect roughly four in ten runs to end in a fall under this disturbance level even with a perfect map, and design the body to survive falling.

What would move it. More push recovery from the balance layer: a higher residual authority (Rule 3, at the cost of tracking), a heavier or lower body, or a push-aware planner that keeps extra distance only on the side a push would come from. None of these is tested yet. A steady pull needs a steady lean against a force the controller cannot see, which may need integral action; a policy with a 240 ms observation history was trained to test this and did not lift the pull limit (S117).

Small robot. The same push is a larger fraction of body weight; pushes in this study scale with weight, so scale the expectation with the robot.

What is not on this page. Flat-ground and standing base fall rates (being re-measured with the fixed contact model, S98), held-out terrain (a small drop of about 0.06, S76, S84), and the open scale study (S93, S95) are results, not rules yet.
