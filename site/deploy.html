From the simulator to a ROS 2 robot

The recommended controller is exported as a ROS 2 package, ros2/ballbot_control: a small numpy core that reproduces, tick for tick, what the controller did in training, wrapped in two thin ROS nodes for Nav2. It was built and tested on a machine without ROS, against recorded simulator data. There is no physical robot.

default bundle g39_goalcentre_s0S118, S121, S122ros2/README.md
  • What exists. A pure-Python control core (no ROS imports, enforced by a test), a controller node at 100 Hz and an estimator node, a launch file, parameters, and exported bundles of the recommended controller g39 (default) and the earlier g34.
  • What was checked. Replaying 13,299 recorded simulator ticks of g39 across all 11 designs, the core reproduces the simulator's observations, actions and wheel torques to float32 rounding (largest torque difference 1.3e-3 N m); 37 tests run without ROS.
  • What was not. No colcon build, no ROS run, no Nav2 integration, no timing on a robot computer, no hardware of any kind.
  • It changed the study. Re-implementing every input found two quantities that were not what they were meant to be; one fix produced the recommended controller (S118), the other is training now (S122).
Numbers on this page are quoted from ros2/README.md and the study log; site/build_data.py checks each quoted figure against that file.
How it fits together

Architecture

Nav2 plans and sends velocity commands at its own rate; the controller node shapes them with the same motion layer the policy was trained with, and runs the balance controller at 100 Hz.

Does it compute the same thing?

Parity with the simulator

The parity test feeds the core the simulator's exact state from a recorded rollout (33 worlds, three per design, 600 ticks, randomisation and pushes on, about half the episodes in goal mode) and compares every intermediate quantity. The simulator runs float32 on the GPU, the core float64, so differences of 1e-4 accumulate over 6 s.

The exported actor files agree with the training network on 29,700 recorded observations to below 1e-6 (TorchScript exactly, numpy 2.4e-07, ONNX 7.2e-07 for g39). A whole controller tick (estimator, motion layer, observation, network, LQR, safety) costs about 0.7 ms in numpy on the development PC; an ARM board will be slower.

What re-implementing it found

Two inputs that were not what they seemed

The goal point swung around the ball (S118)

The goal logic used the ball's body origin, 0.14 m from its centre, which circles the centre as the ball rolls. A real robot would have had to dead-reckon the ball's orientation to reproduce it. Retraining with the goal on the true centre gave g39: hold error 3.6 to 4.9 cm instead of 11.4 to 13.6 cm, and removed that need.

Chapter 10c of the results

The authority dead band read a swinging speed (S122, in progress)

In training, the adaptive-authority dead band compares the ball body-origin speed with 0.05 m/s; on a robot only the centre speed can be estimated, so near zero commands the authority can switch at different moments. A retraining on the centre speed (g41) matched g39 on every number for seed 0, as expected, since its value is consistency, not accuracy; seeds 1 and 2 are retraining, and the bundle stays g39 until three seeds agree. Until then this mismatch is a known open issue of the deployment.

What is known so far

A third quirk is reproduced on purpose: observation entries 9 to 11, meant as motor states, are base velocities and a body rate divided by the torque scale. The trained policies depend on them, so the core computes them the same way; a future retraining should use wheel rates, which a robot measures directly (S118 note).

Starts in HOLD

Zero torque until enabled, and enabling needs the robot upright (tilt under 3 deg) with fresh data; it resets the controller exactly like the start of a training episode.

Faults latch

Tilt above 30 deg, no sensor data within 50 ms, or a non-finite torque or state: FAULT, zero torque, latched until cleared and re-enabled. Torque is always clamped to the trained 30 N m limit.

Command timeout stops and holds

No /cmd_vel for 0.5 s sets the desired velocity to zero and, optionally, holds position at the braking point. An upright ballbot must keep balancing, so a timeout never cuts torque.

Try it

Build, launch and test

With ROS 2 and Nav2 (not yet exercised)

colcon build --packages-select ballbot_control
source install/setup.bash
ros2 launch ballbot_control ballbot_control.launch.py
ros2 service call /ballbot/enable std_srvs/srv/SetBool "{data: true}"

Use a holonomic Nav2 controller (MPPI with the Omni model, or DWB with lateral velocity) with limits at or below the motion layer's: acceleration 0.4 m/s2, braking 0.5 m/s2, yaw rate up to 1 rad/s.

Without ROS (what was actually run)

cd ros2/ballbot_control
python -m pytest -q
python test/parity_replay.py g39_goalcentre_s0
python test/sim_closed_loop.py --speed 0.3

The closed-loop check uses CPU MuJoCo without noise, latency, arrestors or an actuator model; it balanced and held after a command timeout with either bundle, but it is a different plant from the training simulator, so its speeds are not a comparison of the two policies.

What was not verified. No ROS build or run: colcon, launch, executors, QoS, TF, Nav2 and timing were never exercised; the nodes ran only against stand-in ROS classes. No hardware: motor signs, encoder directions, IMU mounting, torque mapping, driver latency, sensor noise and compute timing are all unverified. On a robot the state comes from the estimator, not the simulator; two parts of that mapping are approximate (the gyro is 2 ms newer than the rate the policy saw, and base velocities are reconstructed from the ball centre with rigid offsets, 1 cm/s median but 0.3 m/s at the 99th percentile during impacts). Python is not real-time; for a robot, run the tick in a real-time thread, port it to a ros2_control plugin, or run the balance loop on the microcontroller (about 0.66 M multiply-adds per tick).
