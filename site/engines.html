Does the result depend on the engine?

Physics engines

The early study ran on CPU MuJoCo. The generalist study trains and evaluates on MuJoCo-Warp on the GPU. This page is the check behind that move: same robot, same controller, do the engines agree, and is the GPU faster? Simulation only.

Verdicts

One engine is a plausible accelerator, the other is a cross-check

MuJoCo-Warp (GPU): go with caveats

Same contact model as the CPU engine. With the LQR, speed ratios agree within 0.01, and 94.6 % of speed runs and 96.1 % of push runs agree on fall against no fall over 17 designs. About 260 times the throughput at 4096 worlds. A policy trained on it transfers to the CPU engine (below).

Isaac Sim (PhysX): no-go as the main engine

PhysX has no anisotropic friction, so the wheels needed a fragile roller-ring emulation. It reproduces nominal tracking but not where the robot falls: at 1.1 times speed MuJoCo falls and PhysX holds. No large speedup was shown.

What the gap means. Warp reproduces the headline behaviour, but its trajectory error is 25 to 100 times the CPU version-to-version spread. 17-design sweep

The design ranking is preserved on both engines. Tall-centre-of-mass and 1.5x-scale robots are robust. Low-centre, high-ball-mass and light-ball designs are fragile. The half-scale robot fails on both.

Details: what the designs vary, and the largest disagreement

Designs vary contact angle, scale, mass distribution, rotor inertia, motor torque and wheel friction (the two friction variants were made for this test). The largest disagreement is the 0.25 friction variant (CPU 4 of 4 falls at 0.4, Warp 1 of 4); the cause is not known. Per-design table: outputs/study/mjx_port/sweep_cpu_vs_warp_wide.csv.

Training a policy on the GPU engine

A small PPO test ran the whole loop on the GPU. 60 million steps took 7 minutes (about 140,000 steps per second); the CPU runs reach 5 million in hours. The policy was then evaluated on both engines.

Run wp_nom_q_s0, log entry S21. Randomisation: observation noise, latency, torque and pushes on; mass, friction and damping not randomised. One seed. 4 trials per speed, 16 pushes per level.

    MuJoCo CPU vs Isaac Sim (PhysX)

    Where the physics models differ

    How to read it. Controller: the LQR, nominal design. PhysX has no anisotropic friction, so each wheel is a ring of 16 passive rollers. Reduced protocol (2 headings, 4 pushes per level) on both engines. The report does not state randomisation or noise; no training seeds apply. Source: experiments/isaac_port/REPORT.md.

    Metric, nominal designMuJoCoPhysX
    Speed ratio at 0.40.9870.975
    Speed ratio at 0.81.0301.013
    Wheel-ball normal forcewithin about 5 %
    Open-loop pulse tilt difference0.4 deg RMS
    • Failure boundaries differ. At 1.1 times speed MuJoCo falls (all runs in 8 of 9 designs) while PhysX holds. Fall counts agree in only 1 of 9 designs at 1.1 and 7 of 9 at 0.8. In the 1x push PhysX falls more in 5 of 9 designs and less in none.
    • The contact-angle ranking is reproduced: the 66 degree design survives pushes and the 41 degree design does not.
    • The emulation is fragile. For the same run 2 of 4 roller-ring configurations held balance and 2 fell; a naive import (isotropic friction, capsule wheels) loses balance after 0.39 s.
    • Speed: at 4096 environments about 15,000 control steps per second, roughly 10 times one MuJoCo process.
    Details: what was not finished, and the cost
    • Not finished in PhysX: 8 of the 17 designs (including the half-scale and light-ball ones). Friction forces from the PhysX contact report read about 15 times lower than MuJoCo's, so only normal forces were compared.
    • Speed floor: a 35 to 59 ms floor per physics step that may be contention with training (not resolved).
    • Cost on disk: the install is a 7.5 GB download and a 16 GB environment.
    Reading. PhysX is an independent engine for sanity checks, not a replacement: it disagrees exactly where the study looks, at the point where the controller falls. MuJoCo-Warp keeps the MuJoCo contact model.
