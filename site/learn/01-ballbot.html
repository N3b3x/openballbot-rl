What a ballbot is and why it is hard

A ballbot is a tall body balanced on a single ball. Motors inside the body turn the ball, and the only way to stay up is to keep rolling the ball back under the body. This lesson builds the intuition for why that is hard, using the real numbers of the simulated robot in this project.

Simulation only, no hardwareModel: scripts/study/ballbot_model.py

You will be able to

  • describe the parts of a ballbot and the two numbers that make it hard to control: an unstable pole and a non-minimum-phase zero;
  • explain what "the tilt doubles every 0.145 s" means and where it comes from;
  • predict how moving the centre of mass changes the fall, and why the answer is not the one a broomstick suggests.

1.1Meet the robot

The simulated robot in this study has a 9 cm radius ball of 0.17 kg and a 3.85 kg body whose centre of mass (COM) sits 13 cm above the centre of the ball, 4.0 kg in total. Three omni-wheels press on the ball from above, 51 degrees from its top. Each omni-wheel drives the ball along its own direction and slides freely across it on small rollers, so together the three can spin the ball about any horizontal axis (and turn the body about the vertical).

1.2An inverted pendulum that can roll

Look at the robot from the side and it is two rigid parts: the ball, which rolls without slipping, and the body, which can tilt. Two angles describe it: the ball's rotation φ (so the ball has moved x = rφ) and the body's tilt θ. The motors apply a torque τ between body and ball: whatever turns the ball forward pushes the body backward by the same amount.

Writing the kinetic and potential energy of both parts and applying Lagrange's equations gives two coupled equations (derived symbolically in derive_planar()):

M(θ) [φ'', θ'']T = [τ + …,  −τ + ma g l sin θ + …]T   // gravity only acts on the tilt

Gravity appears only in the tilt equation, with a positive sign: the further the body leans, the harder gravity pulls it over. Linearise around upright and the system has four poles (the rates of its natural motions). One of them is positive, p = 4.79 1/s. A positive pole means a motion that grows by itself: left alone, the tilt grows like ept and doubles every ln 2 / p = 0.145 s.

Two things in that figure are worth a second look.

The wheels slow the fall. A body on a fixed pivot would fall at 6.94 1/s. The ballbot falls at 4.79 1/s, slower, because tipping the body forward has to spin the three motor rotors through the wheels, and their reflected inertia (about 0.10 kg m2, roughly 190 times the ball's own) resists the tilt rate. In the same model with that rotor inertia and the wheel damping set to zero the pole would be 11.05 1/s. This is a model calculation from ballbot_planar_model.json, not a separate study entry.

A taller body does not simply fall more slowly. For a fixed-pivot pendulum, raising the COM lowers the rate. For the ballbot the rate barely moves over the whole range (4.5 to 4.8 1/s in the design sweep) because the body's inertia and the rolling ball change together. What does change is how much the robot must lean to accelerate, the subject of lesson 2.

1.3The second difficulty: a zero on the wrong side

Instability alone is manageable: a fast enough controller can catch a falling body. The ballbot has a second property that limits how well any controller can do. To roll forward, the body must first lean forward (gravity then pulls it along). But the only way to make the body lean forward is to roll the ball backward under it. So every forward move starts with a small backward move.

In control terms the transfer function from motor torque to ball position has a zero at z = +5.78 1/s, in the right half of the complex plane: a non-minimum-phase zero. Its value has a closed form that depends only on the body (it does not depend on the drive):

z = √( ma g l / (Ia + ma l2 + ma l r) ) = 5.78 1/s

The closer the zero is to the unstable pole, the worse the best achievable robustness. A classic bound (Skogestad) says the peak of the sensitivity function is at least (z + p) / |z − p|; with z/p = 1.21 that is 10.7, a structurally poor margin for any controller (S16). This is why the rest of the course spends so much effort on smooth commands, robust training and honest evaluation.

Try it

In the fall race, move the COM from 13 cm to 22 cm. Does the ballbot fall faster, slower, or about the same? And the fixed-pivot body?

Show the answer

The ballbot's rate barely changes (4.79 to 4.63 1/s in the readout; the design generator gives 4.63 for the 0.22 m variant), while the fixed-pivot pendulum slows clearly (6.94 to about 6.1 1/s). In the full simulation the taller body was in fact easier to control (S119: falls while standing 0.7 percent at the nominal height, 0.3 percent at 0.22 m), mainly because it needs less lean per unit of acceleration.

Check yourself

The unstable pole is 4.79 1/s. If the body starts 1 degree off upright with the motors off, roughly how long until it is 8 degrees off?

Why

8 degrees is three doublings of 1 degree, and one doubling takes ln 2 / 4.79 = 0.145 s, so about 0.43 s. A controller has a fraction of a second to react, and it runs every 10 ms in this project.

1.4Key takeaways

  • A ballbot is an inverted pendulum whose pivot (the ball) the motors move. Upright is an equilibrium, but an unstable one.
  • The nominal robot's unstable pole is 4.79 1/s: an uncorrected tilt doubles every 0.145 s.
  • The drive's reflected rotor inertia slows the fall compared with a fixed pivot (4.79 against 6.94 1/s), and the rate is almost independent of COM height.
  • A right-half-plane zero at 5.78 1/s forces the robot to move backward before it can move forward, and caps the robustness of any controller (sensitivity peak at least 10.7).
