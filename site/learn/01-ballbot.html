What a ballbot is and why it is hard

A ballbot is a tall body balanced on one ball. It stays up only by rolling the ball back under itself, all the time.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • name the parts of a ballbot;
  • explain the two numbers that make it hard: an unstable pole and a non-minimum-phase zero;
  • say why a taller body does not fall more slowly here.

1.1Meet the robot

Three omni-wheels inside the body press on the ball and can spin it about any horizontal axis. The ball touches the floor at one point. Nothing else holds the robot up.

1.2Two reasons it is hard

It falls fast. Left alone, the tilt grows by itself. Control engineers call this an unstable pole. Here it is 4.79 1/s: the tilt doubles every 0.145 s.

It starts the wrong way. To lean forward, the robot must first roll the ball backward. This is a non-minimum-phase zero, at 5.78 1/s. No controller can remove it.

The two numbers are close together, and that is the real problem. A classic bound says the closer they are, the less robust any controller can be (S16).

The equations behind the two numbers

Seen from the side, the robot is two rigid parts: a ball that rolls without slipping (rotation φ, so it has moved x = rφ) and a body that tilts (θ). The motors apply a torque τ between them. Lagrange's equations give two coupled equations (derive_planar()):

M(θ) [φ'', θ'']T = [τ + …,  −τ + ma g l sin θ + …]T   // gravity only acts on the tilt

Linearised around upright, the system has four poles. One is positive, p = 4.79 1/s, so the tilt grows like ept and doubles every ln 2 / p = 0.145 s.

The zero has a closed form that depends only on the body, not on the drive:

z = √( ma g l / (Ia + ma l2 + ma l r) ) = 5.78 1/s

The bound (Skogestad): the peak of the sensitivity function is at least (z + p) / |z − p|. With z/p = 1.21 that is 10.7 (S16).

1.3Race it against a broomstick

A body on a fixed pivot would fall at 6.94 1/s. The ballbot falls at 4.79 1/s. It is slower because tipping the body has to spin the three motor rotors through the wheels.

Raising the centre of mass slows a broomstick. It barely changes the ballbot: 4.5 to 4.8 1/s over the whole design sweep.

Why the wheels slow the fall

The rotors' reflected inertia is about 0.10 kg m2, roughly 190 times the ball's own, and it resists the tilt rate. With that inertia and the wheel damping set to zero the pole would be 11.05 1/s. This is a model calculation from ballbot_planar_model.json, not a separate study entry.

Try it

In the fall race, move the COM from 13 cm to 22 cm. Does the ballbot fall faster, slower, or about the same? And the fixed-pivot body?

Show the answer

The ballbot's rate barely changes (4.79 to 4.63 1/s), while the fixed-pivot pendulum slows clearly (6.94 to about 6.1 1/s). In the full simulation the taller body was in fact easier to control (S119: falls while standing 0.7 percent at the nominal height, 0.3 percent at 0.22 m), mainly because it needs less lean per unit of acceleration (lesson 2).

Check yourself

The unstable pole is 4.79 1/s. The body starts 1 degree off upright, motors off. Roughly how long until it is 8 degrees off?

Why

8 degrees is three doublings of 1 degree, and one doubling takes 0.145 s, so about 0.43 s. The controller runs every 10 ms in this project.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • A ballbot is an inverted pendulum whose pivot (the ball) the motors move.
  • Unstable pole 4.79 1/s: an uncorrected tilt doubles every 0.145 s.
  • Zero at 5.78 1/s: it must move backward before it can move forward.
  • The two are close, which caps the robustness of any controller (sensitivity peak at least 10.7).
