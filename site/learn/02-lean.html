Lean to accelerate

A ballbot accelerates by leaning, and to lean it must first roll the ball the wrong way. Two consequences, one law and one limit, explain most of the control design that follows.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • state the lean law and compute the lean needed for a given acceleration;
  • explain the backward dip of a step response through the non-minimum-phase zero;
  • say why the stack slews commands instead of passing steps to the balance controller.

2.1To go forward, fall forward

A wheeled robot accelerates by pushing its wheels against the ground. A ballbot cannot do that: the ball is its only contact, and a torque on the ball just pushes the body the other way. The way it speeds up is by leaning. When the body's centre of mass sits ahead of the contact point, gravity pulls the whole robot forward, and the controller rolls the ball to follow it.

This has an exact form. Add the two equations of motion from lesson 1: the motor torque and the wheel damping appear with opposite signs in the two equations, so they cancel and what is left contains no motor at all. For small tilt and a body that is not turning:

sin θ = κ a,   κ = ((mk + ma) r2 + Ik + ma l r) / (ma g l r)

For the nominal robot κ = 0.176 rad per m/s2, which is 10.1 degrees of lean for every 1 m/s2 of acceleration. It is a statement about gravity and mass, not about the motors: a stronger motor cannot change how far the robot has to lean to accelerate, it can only get there faster.

2.2Why it moves backwards first

Look at the zoomed plot. Right after the command the ball moves a little backward, then reverses. To make the body lean forward, the controller must roll the ball backward under it (the tilt equation is driven by the ball's acceleration). Only once the body is leaning can the ball roll forward to chase it. This is the non-minimum-phase zero of lesson 1 seen in the time domain: the response begins in the wrong direction.

The size of the dip depends on how hard you ask. A gentle command produces a dip of a centimetre or so; an abrupt one makes it several times larger and demands a larger lean. That is why the deployed stack never passes raw steps to the balance controller. It slews the reference velocity at a fixed acceleration (0.5 m/s2 in the deployed LQR, a_max) and, as lesson 5 shows, shapes the commands before they get there.

Set the speed command to 1.0 m/s and the acceleration limit to 4 m/s2 (a nearly raw step). Compare the backward dip and the peak tilt with the default. Then lower the limit to 0.5.

Which setting would a robot with a 30 degree tilt cutoff survive?

Show the answer

In this planar model the raw step makes the dip several times larger and drives the tilt well beyond 30 degrees (about 33 degrees at a limit of 2 m/s2 for a 1 m/s command, more at 4), which is where the deployed safety supervisor cuts the torque (lesson 10). At 0.5 m/s2 the tilt stays near the 10 degrees the lean law predicts for the acceleration actually achieved. Slow commands are not timidity: they are what keeps the lean inside what the robot can recover from.

2.3How much lean does each design need?

Because κ depends on masses and lengths only, it changes little across the 11 designs the controller was trained on. A heavier ball needs a bit more lean, a higher body a bit less. The lean also sets the robot's top acceleration: with the traction-limited recoverable tilt of 20.6 degrees, the nominal robot's lean-limited maximum acceleration is 1.98 m/s2 (S119 physics table), well below what the motors could deliver (113 m/s2 by the torque limit alone).

Check yourself

A designer doubles the motor torque limit. What happens to the lean needed for 1 m/s2?

Why

The torque cancels in the sum of the two equations, so κ depends on masses and lengths only. Stronger motors change how fast the robot can reach the lean and how well it recovers (lesson 8), not how much lean it needs.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Acceleration needs lean: about 10.1 degrees per m/s2 for the nominal robot, and the law has no motor term.
  • A step command is answered by a small move in the wrong direction first, then the lean builds and the ball follows.
  • The harder the acceleration the larger the dip and the lean, so the command must be limited and shaped.
  • Lean sets the top acceleration (1.98 m/s2 nominal), far below what the motors alone could give.
