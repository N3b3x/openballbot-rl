Lean to accelerate

A ballbot speeds up by leaning. To lean, it first rolls the ball the wrong way. One law and one limit follow from that.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • state the lean law and use it;
  • explain the backward dip after a command;
  • say why commands are slewed, never stepped.

2.1To go forward, fall forward

A wheeled robot pushes against the ground. A ballbot cannot: a torque on the ball just pushes the body the other way. So it leans, gravity pulls it forward, and the ball rolls to keep up.

The law has no motor term. A stronger motor does not reduce the lean. It only gets there faster.

The motors alone could give 113 m/s2. The lean allows 1.98 m/s2. Lean is the limit, not torque.

Where the law comes from

Add the two equations of motion of lesson 1. Motor torque and wheel damping appear with opposite signs, so they cancel. For small tilt and a body that is not turning:

sin θ = κ a,   κ = ((mk + ma) r2 + Ik + ma l r) / (ma g l r)

For the nominal robot κ = 0.176 rad per m/s2.

2.2Why it moves backward first

The dip grows with how hard you ask. A gentle command dips about a centimetre. An abrupt one dips several times more and needs a bigger lean.

So the stack never passes a raw step to the balance controller. It slews the speed reference at 0.5 m/s2 (a_max), and lesson 5 shapes commands further.

Try it

Set the speed command to 1.0 m/s and the acceleration limit to 4 m/s2 (nearly a raw step). Compare the dip and the peak tilt with the default. Then lower the limit to 0.5.

Which setting would a robot with a 30 degree tilt cutoff survive?

Show the answer

In this planar model the raw step makes the dip several times larger and drives the tilt well beyond 30 degrees (about 33 degrees at a limit of 2 m/s2 for a 1 m/s command, more at 4), which is where the deployed safety supervisor cuts the torque (lesson 10). At 0.5 m/s2 the tilt stays near the 10 degrees the lean law predicts for the acceleration actually achieved. Slow commands are what keeps the lean inside what the robot can recover from.

2.3Every design leans about the same

The law depends on masses and lengths only. Across the 11 training designs it changes little: a heavier ball needs a bit more lean, a higher body a bit less.

Check yourself

A designer doubles the motor torque limit. What happens to the lean needed for 1 m/s2?

Why

The torque cancels in the sum of the two equations, so κ depends on masses and lengths only. Stronger motors change how fast the robot can reach the lean and how well it recovers (lesson 8), not how much lean it needs.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Acceleration needs lean: about 10.1 degrees per m/s2, with no motor term.
  • A command is answered by a small move the wrong way, then the lean builds and the ball follows.
  • Harder acceleration means a larger dip and lean, so commands are limited and shaped.
  • Lean sets the top acceleration (1.98 m/s2 nominal), far below what the motors could give.
