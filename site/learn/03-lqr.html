LQR balance control

A balance controller is a weighted sum of a few measurements. LQR picks the weights. It balances well and tracks speed exactly, and it stops being enough on a messy world.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • say what each gain does;
  • explain what LQR optimises and how Q and R trade accuracy against effort;
  • recognise a loop that is unstable, ringing or saturated.

3.1The loop

The governor is the slew limit of lesson 2: it never lets the reference speed change faster than 0.5 m/s2. An integral on speed removes the steady error that wheel damping causes.

3.2Three gains, three jobs

LQR (linear quadratic regulator) chooses the gains by an optimisation. You say how much you dislike each error (Q) and how much you dislike effort (R). It returns the gains.

Here Q = diag(10-6, 50, 1, 1) and R = 1. LQR turns "how much do I care" into gains. It does not tell you what to care about.

The formula

The state is x = (ball angle, tilt, ball rate, tilt rate). The controller sets

τ = −K x = −(K1 φ + K2 θ + K3 (φ' − φ'ref) + K4 θ')

LQR minimises the sum over time of xT Q x + τT R τ, at the 10 ms control period.

Try it

Reset the gains. (1) Push at 40 N, then reduce the wheel torque limit until the robot falls. (2) Put the tilt-rate gain at ×0.3. (3) Set the effort weight R to 10 and the tilt weight to 1000.

Show the answer

(1) A single push needs only a modest torque; the limit at which it fails is visible in the widget (this planar model, one push at one instant). It matches the design sweep qualitatively: the weak-motor design (6 N m) mostly balances (13 percent of standing robots fell), while 3 N m falls about half the time even standing (lesson 8, S119). (2) With too little damping the poles move toward the imaginary axis and the response rings. (3) Heavier tilt weights and cheaper effort raise the gains and speed the recovery; heavier effort weights lower them and the robot reacts lazily.

3.3Where LQR alone stops

The LQR is linear. It knows nothing about friction, slip, latency, terrain, sensor noise, or the 11 different robots the project trains on.

Check yourself

In the widget, you set the tilt gain to 0. What do you expect, and why?

Why

Without the tilt term nothing pushes the ball back under a leaning body. A damper alone slows the fall but cannot reverse it, and the open-loop pole at 4.79 1/s remains in the right half plane (the pole plot shows it).

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Torque is a weighted sum of tilt, tilt rate and ball rate. The tilt gain is the spring that tames the unstable pole.
  • LQR picks the gains from weights on error and effort: Q = diag(1e-6, 50, 1, 1), R = 1.
  • Alone, it balances and tracks speed exactly but fails often at goals on a randomised simulator (32 percent falls).
  • Torque limits matter: the loop works only while the motors can supply what it asks.
