Lesson 5 of 108 minCommands

Commands that feel good

A planner can write commands a ballbot cannot follow. A small layer of limits fixes the commands. It only works if the learned controller is trained with it.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • list what the motion-reference layer does;
  • explain why abrupt commands hurt this robot;
  • say why the layer must be present during training.

5.1The problem

A planner knows nothing about leaning. Passing its raw commands straight to the balance controller goes wrong in three ways (S105):

It swaysThe command turned smoothly (1.6 rad/s RMS). The ball's real heading swung at 8.1 rad/s RMS.
It flipsOvershooting a goal reverses the command by 180 degrees. Reversing needs the opposite lean, which takes time (lesson 2).
It huntsNothing holds position at the goal, so the robot circles a goal it has missed.

5.2The fix: a motion-reference layer

The five limits and their settings
StageLimit
Speed slewaccelerate at most 0.4 m/s2, brake at most 0.5 m/s2
Turn rateat most min(1.5 rad/s, alat / speed), alat = 0.35 m/s2
Reversala heading more than 90 degrees away is reached by braking first
Arrivalspeed capped at √(2 abrk (d − dstop))
Holdwithin 6 cm and slow: gain 0.8, speed cap 0.06 m/s, dead band 1.5 cm
Try it

Choose the zigzag scenario. Raise the turn-rate limit to 4 rad/s and the lateral limit to 1 m/s2. What happens to the layer's heading rate? Then choose the 180 degree turn and lower the braking limit to 0.2.

Show the answer

With the limits relaxed the layer passes most of the zigzag through and its heading rate climbs toward the raw one: the smoothing comes from the limits, not from the layer existing. In the navigation test it was the same: raising the turn limit from 1.5 to 2 rad/s brought the path wobble back to the no-layer level (1.72 against 1.79), while accelerating and braking harder cost nothing (S114). On the turn-around, a lower braking limit makes the robot take longer to stop before it reverses.

5.3Train with it, do not bolt it on

The same layer was tested two ways. The result flipped.

0.15 → 0.31goal falls, layer added to an unchanged policy
S106, checkpoint g26
0.15 → 0.11 to 0.12goal falls, policy trained with the layer
S110, runs g29_mref
1.19 → 0.38command reversals per run, trained with the layer
S110

The old policy had only seen raw, piecewise-constant commands. Slow ramps and a hold loop were new to it. The inputs seen in training are part of the controller.

In navigation with a planner and the ToF sensor, the path wobbles a quarter less (heading-rate RMS 1.31 against 1.79 rad/s) and a route takes 0.3 s longer (S114).

Check yourself

The layer shapes the command better, but on an unchanged policy goal falls doubled. What is the most likely reason?

Why

That is the diagnosis in S106: the policy was trained on piecewise-constant commands and a raw goal rule. Retraining with the layer in the loop (S110) turned the same layer from a loss into a gain.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Raw commands make the ballbot sway, flip and hunt.
  • The layer slews speed, limits the turn rate, brakes before reversals, brakes to goals and holds position.
  • Added to an unchanged policy it doubled goal falls (S106). Trained with, it improved arrival and hold (S110).
  • On navigation it cuts path wobble by about a quarter at a cost of 0.3 s per route. The turn-rate limit is the part that matters.
