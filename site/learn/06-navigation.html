Sensing and navigation

A ballbot cannot roll over a 5 cm step, so it has to see and go around. This lesson builds the stack that does it: a small depth sensor, an inflated map, a wavefront planner, and a bump fallback.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • say what the sensor measures and which zones become obstacles;
  • explain the wavefront planner, obstacle inflation and the carrot follower;
  • describe the contact-escape fallback and read the navigation results honestly.

6.1Why the robot needs to see at all

A ballbot rolls on a ball. A step taller than a few centimetres is a wall to it. A simplified quasi-static model (S88) says that for a ball of radius r and floor friction coefficient μ the tallest edge it can roll over is about r(1 − cos(arctan μ)); with μ near 1 that is 0.29 r, or 2.6 cm for the 9 cm ball (friction varies from about 0.4 to 1.2, so the limit varies too). In the simulation, steps and bumps above roughly 2.4 cm made 94 to 98 percent of the nominal robots fall at 0.3 m/s, and approach speed was not the driver (S87, S88). Neither a perfect height map nor a depth sensor changed that. What helps is not climbing: routing around what cannot be crossed.

That takes three ingredients: a sensor that sees obstacles ahead, a planner that turns them into a path, and a fallback for when the sensor misses something.

6.2The sensor: a small depth camera on the head

The simulated sensor is modelled on a multizone time-of-flight (ToF) chip (the VL53L9CX: 54 by 42 zones, about 55 by 42 degrees of view, 5 cm to 9 m). To keep the simulation fast the study bins it to 18 by 14 zones, pitches it 25 degrees down and mounts it 0.15 m above the base origin. Each zone reports a range; the noise is proportional to the range (0.5 percent) and 2 percent of zones drop out and read as the maximum range (4 m). Any zone whose hit point is more than 3 cm above the ground becomes an obstacle in the planner's map.

Try it

Set the block height to 8 cm (the height used in the navigation tests) and slide the distance from 0.3 to 2.5 m. How does the number of zones that see the block change? Then set the pitch to 0 degrees and repeat.

Show the answer

At the default pitch the block is seen at every distance in the slider, because the top beams point only a few degrees below horizontal; the nearer it is, the more zones hit it. Closer than about 0.4 m everything is under the lowest beam: a blind zone around the robot. With the pitch at 0 the lowest beam is about 20 degrees down, so an 8 cm block closer than roughly 0.9 m passes under the beams unseen. The study aimed the sensor down for that reason, and also aimed it at the planner target rather than the robot heading (lesson 7 of the Lessons page of the main site).

6.3The planner: a wavefront and a carrot

The map is a grid around the robot. Obstacles are inflated by the robot's half-width plus a margin (0.22 m in the study), so the robot can be treated as a point. A wavefront (breadth-first propagation, 8-connected) starts at the goal and writes into every free cell its distance to the goal. Following the field downhill from any cell gives a shortest path.

Following the field one cell at a time is jerky: the best neighbour flips with tiny map changes. The study's fix is a carrot: descend the field about 0.3 m ahead and steer toward that point, with the heading limited to 2 rad/s (the smooth follower of S90). This cut the heading oscillation of the command from about 14 rad/s RMS for the jerky planner to about 1.7 (S89, S104). The command then goes through the motion layer of lesson 5.

Try it

Load "Wall with a gap" and Run with inflation 2. Then raise inflation to 3 or 4 and Run again. Finally, switch to Blind + bump escape with inflation 2.

Show the answer

With inflation 2 the 70 cm gap leaves a 20 cm wide free lane and the robot passes through. At 3 cells the inflated walls close the gap and the planner reports no path: a margin that is too generous makes a feasible route disappear, which is why the margin is 0.22 m and not 0.5 m. In the blind mode the robot drives straight at the wall, stalls, marks cells, backs up and slowly feels its way to the gap. It reaches the goal more often than not, but it takes longer and the bump count shows the cost; on the real simulated robot those bumps are where the falls come from (S109).

6.4When the sensor fails: contact escape

Sensors have blind spots and can break. S109 asked how far a robot gets with no terrain sensing at all, using only the planner, a stall detector and a bump layer in the map (the Nav2 pattern of a costmap bump layer plus back-up recovery). A stall is detected when the ball makes less than 30 percent of the commanded speed for 1 s, or the goal gets no closer by 0.1 m in 4 s, or the tilt passes 15 degrees. Blind straight-line driving reached 9 percent of goals; with the escape layer, 54 percent (S109), and 63 percent on the recommended controller (S115). The weak point is detection: half the robots partly roll onto a block before the stall triggers, and those are the falls (0.26).

Check yourself

The ToF-driven planner reaches 0.87 of goals and the perfect-map planner 0.95. What does that gap tell you?

Why

The perfect map removes every perception error, so the gap between the two is the ceiling on what better perception can give. The remaining 5 percent of failures with a perfect map are falls and stalls of the controller itself (S121). Whether 0.09 is worth a sensor depends on the application, which is the kind of judgement the numbers make possible.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Obstacles above roughly 0.29 r (2.6 cm here) are almost never crossed in the simulation, so the answer is routing, not climbing.
  • An 18 by 14 ToF sensor pitched down 25 degrees, with 0.5 percent range noise and 2 percent dropout, feeds a 3 cm obstacle threshold.
  • A wavefront over an inflated grid gives paths; following a carrot 0.3 m ahead with a turn limit makes them smooth.
  • A bump layer with back-up and re-plan lifts blind navigation from 9 to 54 percent, with ToF at 87 and a perfect map at 95 percent.
