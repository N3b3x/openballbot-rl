Sensing and navigation

A ballbot cannot roll over a tall step. So it has to see obstacles and go around them. Four pieces do that: a small depth sensor, a map, a planner, and a bump fallback.

Simulation only, no hardware

6.2The sensor

The sensor is a simulated multizone time-of-flight (ToF) chip, binned to 18 by 14 zones. Each zone reports a range. A hit more than 3 cm above the ground becomes an obstacle.

Sensor details

Modelled on the VL53L9CX (54 by 42 zones, about 55 by 42 degrees of view, 5 cm to 9 m). Binned to 18 by 14 to keep the simulation fast, pitched 25 degrees down, mounted 0.15 m above the base origin. Noise is 0.5 percent of range; 2 percent of zones drop out and read the maximum range (4 m).

Try it

Set the block height to 8 cm and slide the distance from 0.3 to 2.5 m. How does the number of zones that see the block change? Then set the pitch to 0 degrees and repeat.

Show the answer

At the default pitch the block is seen at every distance in the slider, because the top beams point only a few degrees below horizontal; the nearer it is, the more zones hit it. Closer than about 0.4 m everything is under the lowest beam: a blind zone. With the pitch at 0 the lowest beam is about 20 degrees down, so an 8 cm block closer than roughly 0.9 m passes under the beams unseen. The study aimed the sensor down for that reason, and also aimed it at the planner target rather than the robot heading (see Lessons on the main site).

6.3The planner: a wavefront and a carrot

1. InflateGrow every obstacle by 0.22 m. Now the robot can be treated as a point.
2. WavefrontStart at the goal. Write into every free cell its distance to the goal. Downhill is the shortest path.
3. CarrotAim at a point 0.3 m down the path, turning at most 2 rad/s. This cut command wobble from about 14 to about 1.7 rad/s RMS (S89, S104).
Try it

Load "Wall with a gap" and Run with inflation 2. Then raise inflation to 3 or 4 and Run again. Finally, switch to Blind + bump escape with inflation 2.

Show the answer

With inflation 2 the 70 cm gap leaves a 20 cm wide free lane and the robot passes. At 3 cells the inflated walls close the gap and the planner reports no path: a margin that is too generous makes a feasible route disappear, which is why the margin is 0.22 m and not 0.5 m. In the blind mode the robot drives at the wall, stalls, marks cells, backs up and feels its way to the gap. It takes longer, and on the simulated robot those bumps are where the falls come from (S109).

The gap between ToF and a perfect map is about 0.09. That is the most a better sensor could buy. Under pushes and pulls ToF success drops to 0.54 (S121): pushes near blocks are the hard case that remains.

How a stall is detected

The ball makes less than 30 percent of the commanded speed for 1 s, or the goal gets no closer by 0.1 m in 4 s, or the tilt passes 15 degrees. The weak point is detection: half the robots partly roll onto a block before the stall triggers, and those are the falls (0.26, S115).

Check yourself

The ToF-driven planner reaches 0.87 of goals and the perfect-map planner 0.95. What does that gap tell you?

Why

The perfect map removes every perception error, so the gap is the ceiling on what better perception can give. The remaining 5 percent of failures with a perfect map are falls and stalls of the controller itself (S121).

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Tall obstacles are not climbed (predicted step limit 0.29 r, 2.6 cm here), so the answer is routing.
  • An 18 by 14 ToF sensor pitched down 25 degrees feeds a 3 cm obstacle threshold.
  • A wavefront over an inflated grid gives paths; a carrot 0.3 m ahead with a turn limit makes them smooth.
  • Goals reached: blind 0.09, bump escape 0.54 to 0.63, ToF 0.87, perfect map 0.95.
