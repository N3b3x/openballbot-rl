Knowing where you are

With no GPS and no map, the robot tracks its own motion from wheel encoders and an IMU. In simulation it is good to about 2 percent of the distance driven. Heading is what limits it.

Simulation only, no hardware

7.1The estimator

The Kalman filter smooths noise. It cannot remove a scale error or a heading error. Those two make position drift.

Each stage in detail
  1. Attitude. Integrate the bias-corrected gyro; pull roll and pitch toward the direction of gravity seen by the accelerometer (a complementary filter). Yaw has no such reference.
  2. Gyro bias. Estimated only while the robot stands still (all wheel rates and the gyro near zero), as a slow average: a zero-velocity update.
  3. Ball rate. From the encoders through a calibration matrix; for a ball rolling without slipping the centre moves at v = (ωy r, −ωx r).
  4. Kalman filter on (x, y, vx, vy): predict with the tilt-compensated horizontal acceleration, update with the encoder velocity.

The estimator tracks the ball centre. The simulator's ball body origin sits 14 cm from the centre and swings as the ball rolls (lesson 9). The centre rolls cleanly: median slip 2 cm/s at a median 0.46 m/s.

7.2Why position drifts

Position is speed added up over time, so errors pile up. A heading error of δ puts the position off by about δ times the distance driven since.

Try it

(1) Set the speed scale error to 5 percent and the gyro bias to 0. (2) Set the scale to 0 and the bias to 0.01 rad/s. (3) With the bias at 0.01, switch on the 3 s stops. Which error is easier to see, and which does stopping fix?

Show the answer

A scale error stretches the whole figure but leaves it recognisable; a heading drift rotates and unwinds it, and the error grows faster the longer it runs. Stopping lets the filter measure the bias (about three quarters of it in a 3 s stop) and lowers the heading error by a large factor, but not to zero, and only works if the robot actually stops. S108 found the same: the gyro bias is only re-estimated while standing, and in the logged driving that is rare.

Protocol

128 simulated robots drove 60 s each on a flat map (commands capped at 0.4 m/s, resampled every 3 s, half of them chasing goals). The estimator ran offline on the logged sensor streams. The encoder calibration was fitted on the first 64 robots; results are on the other 64. IMU noise values are typical MEMS figures (gyro bias standard deviation 0.005 rad/s; accelerometer bias 0.03 m/s2), not a specific part. On terrain with steps and drops the error is larger: 3.6 to 3.8 percent of distance, against 2.0 to 2.4 on flat ground (S108 addendum).

Check yourself

After a minute of driving, the realistic-sensor estimate is 22 cm off and 12 degrees off in heading. Which change would reduce position error most?

Why

S108: a 2 percent ball radius error changes the position error almost not at all, while the heading drift turns into most of the position error. The fix has to come from something that observes heading: a better gyro, pauses to re-estimate the bias, or landmark or map matching from the ToF sensor or a camera; in ROS 2, a localiser fused with the odometry (lesson 10).

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Encoders give speed, the IMU gives attitude; a Kalman filter fuses them.
  • Gyro bias is only observable while standing still, so heading drifts: 12 degrees after a minute.
  • Wheel-matrix calibration matters most (13 percent error over the first 2 m uncalibrated); ball radius error hardly matters.
  • Odometry is good for a few metres; longer missions need an absolute heading or position correction.
