Knowing where you are

Without GPS or a map the robot has to track its own motion from wheel encoders and an IMU. It does it to about 2 percent of distance, and the part that limits it is heading.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • list the four stages of the estimator and what each fixes;
  • explain how heading and scale errors turn into position drift;
  • say what a Kalman filter can and cannot remove, and what the stack needs for longer missions.

7.1Odometry from wheel encoders and an IMU

A navigation stack needs to know where the robot is. There is no GPS or camera localiser here, only what a ballbot has on board: three wheel encoders and a 6-axis IMU (a gyroscope and an accelerometer). The estimator of S108 turns them into position, velocity and attitude in four steps, every 10 ms:

  1. Attitude. Integrate the bias-corrected gyro; pull roll and pitch toward the direction of gravity seen by the accelerometer (a complementary filter). Yaw has no such reference: with no compass or camera it drifts with whatever gyro bias remains.
  2. Gyro bias. Estimated only while the robot is standing still (all wheel rates and the gyro near zero), as a slow average. This zero-velocity update is the standard way to observe an IMU bias without an external reference.
  3. Ball rate. The ball's rotation relative to the body comes from the encoders through a calibration matrix; for a ball rolling without slipping the ball centre moves at v = (ωy r, −ωx r).
  4. Kalman filter on (x, y, vx, vy): predict with the tilt-compensated horizontal acceleration, update with the encoder velocity. It smooths noise and quantisation; it cannot remove a scale or heading error, which is what makes position drift.

One detail of the simulator: it logs the position of the ball body origin, which sits 14 cm from the ball centre in this model and swings as the ball rolls. The centre rolls cleanly (median slip 2 cm/s at a median 0.46 m/s), so the estimator tracks the centre (the same origin offset reappears as a measurement artefact in lesson 9).

7.2Why position drifts

Position comes from integrating velocity, so any error in the speed or in the direction of travel accumulates. Two kinds of error matter:

  • Scale error (a wrong wheel calibration or ball radius) stretches the path in proportion: 3 percent too fast means 3 percent too far.
  • Heading error rotates every later step. A heading error of δ makes a position error of about δ times the distance driven since the heading went wrong. Heading drifts because the gyro has a bias that cannot be seen without a reference.
Try it

(1) Set the speed scale error to 5 percent and the gyro bias to 0. (2) Set the scale to 0 and the bias to 0.01 rad/s. (3) With the bias at 0.01, switch on the 3 s stops. Which error is easier to see in the picture, and which does stopping fix?

Show the answer

A scale error stretches the whole figure but leaves it recognisable; a heading drift rotates and unwinds it, and the error grows faster the longer it runs. Stopping lets the filter measure the bias (about three quarters of it in a 3 s stop) and lowers the heading error by a large factor, but not to zero, and only works if the robot actually stops. S108 found the same: heading is the error that grows, the gyro bias is only re-estimated while standing, and in the logged driving that is rare.

7.3What the simulated study measured

S108 drove 128 simulated robots for 60 s each (commands capped at 0.4 m/s, resampled every 3 s, half of them chasing goals) on a flat map, logging the true state and the sensor streams, and ran the estimator offline. The calibration of the encoder matrix was fitted on the first 64 robots and the results are on the other 64. The IMU noise values are typical MEMS figures (gyro bias standard deviation 0.005 rad/s, random walk, noise; accelerometer bias 0.03 m/s2), not a specific part.

Check yourself

After a minute of driving, the realistic-sensor estimate is 22 cm off and 12 degrees off in heading. Which change would reduce position error most?

Why

S108: a 2 percent ball radius error changes the position error almost not at all, while the heading drift turns into most of the position error. The fix has to come from something that observes heading: a better gyro, pauses to re-estimate the bias, or landmark or map matching from the ToF sensor or a camera; in ROS 2, a localiser fused with the odometry (lesson 10).

On terrain with steps and drops the error is a little larger (3.6 to 3.8 percent of distance, against 2.0 to 2.4 on flat ground), because the ball slips and rolls over edges that the flat-ground calibration does not model (S108 addendum).

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Encoders give speed, the IMU gives attitude; a Kalman filter fuses encoder speed with accelerometer prediction.
  • Gyro bias is only observable while standing still, so heading drifts: 12 degrees after a minute.
  • Calibration of the wheel matrix matters most (13 percent error over the first 2 m uncalibrated); ball radius error hardly matters.
  • Odometry is good for a few metres; longer missions need an absolute heading or position correction.
