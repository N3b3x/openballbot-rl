Lesson 8 of 108 minDesign

Designing the robot

The simulation that trains the controller can also ask what a good ballbot looks like. Seven rules came out, and one limit that no controller moved.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • rank the properties that matter;
  • tell a physical limit from a gap in training;
  • estimate the steady pull a robot resists from its body mass.

8.1One question, one property at a time

Which properties of the build does the controller need? The sweep (S119) took the nominal robot and changed one property at a time. Each variant ran 256 randomised simulated robots under five conditions.

The controller in the sweep was g34, the predecessor of the recommended controller. Every variant was new to it.

The nominal robot and the five conditions

Nominal: 9 cm ball of 0.17 kg, a 3.85 kg body with its centre of mass 13 cm above the ball centre, 30 N m peak torque per wheel, rotor inertia 0.005 kg m2, wheel contact 51 degrees from the top. Conditions: holding a goal; holding it while pushed; while pulled with half-strength sustained pulls; driving at 0.3 m/s; reaching goals on terrain. Each design was built from a physical spec and checked with the analytic margins of lesson 1 first.

Try it

Select Ball mass, then Motor torque limit. How far can a designer go before the robot stops balancing? Then select Ball radius and read the warning.

Show the answer

Ball mass: with a ball under about 2 percent of the body mass the robot falls even standing (0.41 at 1.1 percent); the nominal 4.4 percent is good and 9 to 17 percent is better, with no standing falls. Motor torque: half the nominal limit (15 N m) is as good as nominal; at a third the falls rise six times; at 10 to 20 percent the robot cannot hold itself. Ball radius: the 12 cm ball is physically easier (smaller unstable pole), yet the learned controller falls 69 percent because it never trained on another ball size. The LQR alone falls 2 percent there. Some rows measure the training range of the controller as much as the robot.

8.2The seven guidelines

The fine print on torque and centre of mass

Torque: continuous rating about 3 N m; the median torque actually used was 0.73 N m and the 99th percentile 8.1 N m, with short peaks to the limit during recoveries. Centre of mass: higher is better up to 2.4 ball radii. Combinations were not tested: a design that passes every rule is a good candidate, not a proven one.

8.3The pull limit

A steady sideways force needs a steady lean against it. The lean the robot can hold is limited, so the pull it can resist is too.

Stronger training pushes, longer training and a longer controller memory did not change it (S116, S117). Body mass is the only lever found.

Check yourself

A designer wants cheaper motors with one tenth of the nominal torque (3 N m per wheel instead of 30). What does the sweep predict for a robot of the same mass?

Why

Torque actually used is small (median 0.73 N m) but recoveries from pushes need short peaks up to the limit. At 6 N m (a fifth) 13 percent of robots fall standing, at 3 N m about half, and the LQR alone falls just as often, so the limit is physical (S119). Driving at 0.3 m/s still worked at those torques, which shows why standing and pushed conditions belong in the test.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Ball mass matters most, and the planar model misses it: below about 2 percent of the body mass the robot cannot stand.
  • Motor torque: at least half the nominal limit. Below a third, falls rise sharply and the LQR alone fails equally.
  • A taller body is easier; rotor inertia not below nominal; contact angle about 50 degrees.
  • Body mass sets the steady pull resisted, about m g sin 5 degrees (3.3 N nominal). No controller change moved it.
  • Rows outside the training range (the 12 cm ball) measure the controller, not the build: check with the LQR alone.
