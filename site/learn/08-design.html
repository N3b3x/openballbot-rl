Lesson 8 of 1016 minDesign

Designing the robot

The same simulation that trains the controller can ask what a good ballbot looks like. This lesson reads the design sweep: which properties matter, which limits are physical, and which rows only show the controller's training range.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • name the properties the sweep varied and rank their importance;
  • separate a physical limit of a build from a gap in the controller's training;
  • estimate the sustained pull a robot can resist from its body mass.

8.1One question: what must a robot have for this controller to work?

There is no physical robot yet, so the project can ask a design question cheaply: which properties of the build does the controller need? S119 took the nominal robot (9 cm ball of 0.17 kg, a 3.85 kg body with its centre of mass 13 cm above the ball centre, 30 N m peak torque per wheel, rotor inertia 0.005 kg m2, wheel contact 51 degrees from the top) and changed one property at a time. Each new design was built from a physical spec, checked with the analytic margins of lesson 1, and then simulated with 256 randomised robots per case under five conditions: holding a goal, holding it while being pushed, while being pulled with half-strength sustained pulls, driving at 0.3 m/s, and reaching goals on terrain. The controller was the recommended recipe (g34 at the time of the sweep); each design is unseen by it.

Try it

In the explorer, select Ball mass and then Motor torque limit. What does each curve say about how far a designer can go before the robot stops balancing? Then select Ball radius and read the warning.

Show the answer

Ball mass: with a ball under about 2 percent of the body mass the robot falls even standing (0.41 at 1.1 percent); the nominal 4.4 percent is good and 9 to 17 percent is better, with no standing falls. The planar analytic model hardly moves with ball mass, so only the full contact simulation shows this. Motor torque: half the nominal limit (15 N m) is as good as nominal; at a third the falls rise six times; at 10 to 20 percent the robot cannot hold itself. Ball radius: the 12 cm ball is physically easier (smaller unstable pole), yet the learned controller falls 69 percent because it never trained on another ball size. The LQR alone falls 2 percent there. That is the extrapolation caveat: some rows measure the training range of the controller as much as the robot.

8.2The seven guidelines

The sweep's results condense into seven rules (docs/research/design_guidelines.md), in order of importance: a ball mass of at least 4 percent of the body mass; motor peak torque of at least 15 N m per wheel for a 4 kg robot (continuous rating about 3 N m; the median torque actually used was 0.73 N m, the 99th percentile 8.1 N m, with short peaks to the limit during recoveries); rotor inertia not below 0.005 kg m2; a body centre of mass at least the nominal 1.4 ball radii above the ball centre, higher is better up to 2.4; body mass of 3 to 5 kg; a contact angle near 50 degrees; and a ball of 9 cm or larger. A taller body is easier to control, the opposite of what a broomstick suggests, because it needs less lean per acceleration (lesson 2).

8.3The pull limit

One limit does not depend on the controller. A short shove is absorbed completely. A sustained sideways force needs a steady lean against it, and the lean the robot can hold is limited: roughly ma g sin 5°, about 3.3 N for the nominal 3.85 kg body. Beyond that falls rise in proportion to the pull: 0.08 up to 3 N, 0.14 at 1.5 to 6 N, 0.22 at 2.25 to 9 N and 0.36 at 3 to 12 N on flat ground (S116). Stronger training pushes, longer training and a longer controller memory did not change it (S116, S117). Body mass is the only lever found: it sets the pull the robot resists.

Check yourself

A designer wants to cut the cost of a ballbot by using motors with one tenth of the nominal torque (3 N m per wheel instead of 30). What does the sweep predict for a robot of the same mass?

Why

Torque actually used is small (median 0.73 N m) but the recoveries from pushes need short peaks up to the limit. At 6 N m (a fifth) 13 percent of robots fall standing, at 3 N m about half, and the LQR alone falls just as often, so the limit is physical (S119). Interestingly, driving at 0.3 m/s still worked at those torques, which shows why standing and pushed conditions belong in the test.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Ball mass is the strongest single property and the planar analytic model misses it: below about 2 percent of the body mass the robot cannot stand.
  • Motor torque of at least half the nominal limit is needed; below a third falls rise sharply and the LQR alone fails equally.
  • A taller body is easier; rotor inertia should not be lower than nominal; the contact angle should be about 50 degrees.
  • Body mass sets the sustained pull resisted, about m g sin 5 degrees (3.3 N for the nominal body); no controller change moved that limit.
  • Rows outside the training range (the 12 cm ball) measure the controller, not the build: check with the LQR alone.
