Lessons from the study

Some of the most useful results of the study are corrections. This lesson reads four of them as interactive examples: what was measured, why it was wrong, and the habit that would have caught it.

Simulation only, no hardware

You will be able to

  • check that a test measures the point and the conditions you think it does;
  • recognise an exposure artefact and ask whether every subject met the test;
  • decide whether a difference between two runs is a result or seed noise.

9.1Every number has a measurement behind it

The earlier lessons quote many numbers. This one is about how some of them turned out to be wrong, and what each error taught. The study log (docs/research/tracking_study_log.md) keeps the corrections next to the original claims, with the entry that withdrew them. The main site collects the engineering side in its Lessons page (a stale derived field in the physics engine, file names that carry settings, finite maps, reference frames, loaded reference poses, sensor aiming, solver blow-ups). Here are the measurement lessons, each with something to play with.

9.2Lesson A: the point you measure may not be the point you think

The goal logic, the goal observation, the motion layer's hold loop and the goal metrics all used the position of the ball body origin. In the simulator's model the ball geometry sits 0.14 m from that origin, and the origin rotates with the ball. Upright at reset, it swings horizontally by up to the full 0.14 m as the ball rolls. So the "goal" the robot held was a point on a 14 cm circle around its contact point, and it depended on how far the ball had rolled. Hold errors of 6 to 8 cm RMS reported in S110 to S117 were measured at that swinging point; at the true centre the same policies held 11 to 14 cm off. The fix moves all goal logic to the true centre (S118).

9.3Lesson B: exposure, or letting every test subject meet the test

The first test of a speed layer that slows the robot before obstacles seemed to halve obstacle falls (0.38 to 0.15 to 0.17). Then the same layer was tested on the goal task and did not help. The cause: in 15 s episodes the slowed robots had not yet reached the obstacles, so they could not fall on them. Re-running with 40 s episodes, so that every robot reaches its obstacle, the effect shrank to 0.03 to 0.05 (0.38 without, 0.33 with a perfect map, 0.35 with the ToF sensor), mostly on stairs (S103). The same trap had bitten before (S86) and came back for the zigzag test below.

Try it

In the toy, set the slowed speed to 0.12 m/s and move the episode length from 15 s to 45 s. At which length do the two curves meet? What would you check in any test that compares a slow controller with a fast one?

Show the answer

The curves meet when even the slowest robot has had time to reach the farthest obstacle (2.4 m at 0.12 m/s takes 20 s; the curves meet there). The check is: did every subject meet the test? Record how many robots actually reached the feature, and compare falls per robot that did, not per robot started.

9.4Lesson C: the test map decides what the test measures

Two different tests went wrong the same way. The zigzag test (60 degree command switches every 2 s) fell 28 to 33 percent in every recipe and looked like a limit of the controller. But the robot started on a flat tile and ran for 15 s, and the zigzag's net forward travel of about 2 m carried it onto neighbouring terrain. On a fully flat map the same zigzag fell 5 percent; changing the layer's lateral limit changed nothing (S113). The claim "sharp turns are a recipe limit" was withdrawn.

The showcase renders had a similar story. Eight-second clips fell on every terrain tile; the first explanation was "driving off the map edge", which was wrong. The height profiles along the clip paths show a flat pad at each tile's centre and a feature 0.55 to 0.65 m out, followed by harder ones: the clips ran into the next feature (S113 note, corrected on 2026-11-10).

9.5Lesson D: seeds, noise and what counts as a result

Training is random: a different seed gives a different policy. The study repeats recipes over seeds and treats any difference smaller than the spread between seeds of the same recipe as noise. Several early conclusions fell to this rule: the goal-hunting behaviour that motivated the dead band was one seed (S113); the "hard-design weighting" gain disappeared with a third seed (S114); the benefit of tracking weight 3 did not reproduce (S112, S113).

Check yourself

Recipe B scores 0.78 against recipe A's 0.76 on one seed each, and B is cheaper. What should you conclude?

Why

In the data above, seeds of one recipe differ by 0.01 to 0.02 in this metric, as much as the gap. A comparison needs several seeds per recipe and a spread. The study logs single-seed results as such, flags them in the entry, and retracts them when a later seed disagrees.

SummaryKey takeaways

  • Check what a measurement is attached to: the ball body origin swung 14 cm around the true centre and blurred every goal metric (S118).
  • Count the subjects that met the test: slowed robots looked twice as safe because they had not reached the obstacles (S103).
  • The test map is part of the test: the zigzag falls were terrain, not turning (S113).
  • A single seed is not a result: compare differences with the spread among seeds of one recipe (S112 to S114).
  • Keep a ledger of corrections and give each its own entry.
