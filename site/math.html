The robot and its physics

Why a ball-balancing robot is hard, in nine short derivations

From a drawing of the robot to every equation the controller relies on. Each section gives the result first, a picture, and one line on why it matters. The algebra sits under "Derivation" folds.

Derived math (blue)Learned part (violet)Combined controller (teal)Simulation only, no hardware

In short

  1. A ballbot is an inverted pendulum on a ball. Left alone, a lean grows e-fold every 0.21 s.
  2. To go forward it must first roll backward (a non-minimum-phase zero), and to keep accelerating it must lean: \(\sin\theta=\kappa a\), about 10 degrees per m/s².
  3. An LQR on this model, with a velocity governor and a slow integral, tracks speed almost exactly. A small learned residual (authority 0.25) adds robustness.
  4. Scaling the robot by \(s\) scales speeds by \(\sqrt s\) and forces by \(s^3\). Commands and pushes are scaled the same way.
  5. A ball cannot climb a step higher than \(r\,(1-\cos\arctan\mu)\), about 0.29 of its radius: 2.6 cm for the 9 cm ball. That is the slow-approach limit; a run-up helps a little (S126).
  6. Passive arrestors hold the body on the ball with forces through the ball's centre, so they leave the model untouched.
  7. A distance field and a carrot follower steer the robot around what it cannot climb.

1. The robot in one plane

Two angles describe everything

Seen from the side, the robot is a ball with a body balanced on top. Two angles describe it: how far the ball has rolled, and how far the body leans.

Coordinates. \(\varphi\) is the ball rotation (radius \(r\)). \(\theta\) is the body tilt from vertical. The ball rolls without slipping. The body's centre of mass sits a height \(l\) above the ball centre:

$$x=r\varphi,\qquad \text{COM at }\big(x+l\sin\theta,\; l\cos\theta\big).$$

The motor is an internal pair. A torque \(\tau\) on the ball is matched by \(-\tau\) on the body. This one fact explains the wrong-way start in section 2. Rotor inertia \(J\) and damping \(d\) act on the relative angle \(\varphi-\theta\).

2. Why it falls, and why it first rolls the wrong way

From energy to equations of motion

Two facts follow from the equations of motion: the robot falls on its own, and it must first roll the wrong way to start moving.

Derivation: energy and equations of motion

Energy. The ball translates and spins, the body translates with the ball and swings about it, and the rotors spin with the relative rate:

$$\begin{aligned}T&=\tfrac12\big(I_k+m_kr^2\big)\dot\varphi^2+\tfrac12 m_a\big[(r\dot\varphi+l\cos\theta\,\dot\theta)^2+(l\sin\theta\,\dot\theta)^2\big]\\&\quad+\tfrac12 I_a\dot\theta^2+\tfrac12 J\,(\dot\varphi-\dot\theta)^2,\qquad V=m_a\,g\,l\cos\theta .\end{aligned}$$

The bracket expands to \(r^2\dot\varphi^2+2rl\cos\theta\,\dot\varphi\dot\theta+l^2\dot\theta^2\). The middle term couples ball and body: move one and you move the other.

Equations of motion. With the motor pair \((\tau,-\tau)\) and the wheel damping on the relative rate:

$$M(\theta)\begin{pmatrix}\ddot\varphi\\\ddot\theta\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}-m_alr\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2\\-m_agl\sin\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}\big(\tau-d(\dot\varphi-\dot\theta)\big),$$ $$M(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}I_k+J+(m_a+m_k)r^2 & m_alr\cos\theta-J\\ m_alr\cos\theta-J & I_a+J+m_al^2\end{pmatrix}.$$

This is derive_planar() in ballbot_model.py; the live simulator in section 4 integrates exactly these equations.

Near upright, it is a linear system

For small lean the equations become \(\dot s=As+B\tau\) with state \(s=(\varphi,\theta,\dot\varphi,\dot\theta)\). The matrices follow the sliders:

Fact 1: an unstable pole, the fall

A pole is a rate at which the system moves by itself. This robot has one with a positive rate.

Proposition 1 (paper_v2)

With no wheel damping the linearised plant has poles \(\{0,0,\pm\lambda\}\),

$$\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{m_a g l\,M_{11}}{\det M_0}}.$$
Derivation

Why. With \(\tau=0\) and small angles the equations read \(M_0\ddot q=K q\) with \(K=\mathrm{diag}(0,\,m_agl)\). The gravity entry \(m_agl\) is an inverted pendulum: it pushes the lean the way it is already going. Solving \(\det(M_0s^2-K)=0\) gives \(s^2(\det M_0\, s^2-m_aglM_{11})=0\).

For the robot on the sliders: undamped \(\lambda=\) 1/s, with damping \(p=\) 1/s. A lean grows e-fold every  s. A lower centre of mass or a bigger ball slows the fall.

So whatThe controller has about a fifth of a second before a lean has grown by a factor of 2.7. Sensing delay and loop rate matter.

Fact 2: a right-half-plane zero, the wrong-way start

A zero in the right half plane means the output first moves the wrong way. Output: ball position. Input: motor torque.

Proposition 2 (paper_v2)

The transfer function from torque to ball position has a real zero pair \(\pm z\),

$$z=\sqrt{\frac{m_a g l}{I_a+m_al^2+m_alr}},$$

independent of the rotor inertia and the damping. The zero at \(+z\) makes the plant non-minimum phase.

In words. To go forward the body must lean forward. The only way to tip the body is to push on the ball, and the torque is an internal pair. So to lean forward the controller first rolls the ball backward. Every controller shows this reversal; the paper measured 0.3 to 1.7 cm of it in the full simulation.

Derivation

Why, in algebra. Cramer's rule on the linearised equations gives the numerator of \(\hat\varphi/\hat\tau\) as \((M_{22}+M_{12})s^2-m_agl\). The \(J\) and \(d\) terms cancel because both act on the relative coordinate \(\varphi-\theta\).

So whatYou cannot ask this robot for a sudden change of speed. That is why section 4 has a velocity governor and section 5 keeps the learned part small.
One level deeper: the sensitivity floor, and why z and p being close is bad

Feeding back ball position through any stabilising linear controller, an unstable pole \(p\) and a right-half-plane zero \(z\) impose a floor on the peak of the sensitivity function (Skogestad's bound), with \(T\) the total loop delay:

$$\lVert S\rVert_\infty\;\ge\;\frac{z+p}{\lvert z-p\rvert}\,e^{pT}.$$

It blows up as \(z\to p\). Loop shaping also wants the crossover above the pole and below about half the zero, \(p<\omega_c2p\). For this robot \(z/p=\), so the window is . The floor is (about dB) with zero delay and with a 15 ms loop. Across the designs in design_sweep.csv, \(z/p\) ranges from to : no body shape we tried opens the window. 3. The lean law

To accelerate, lean. How much is fixed by the body.

To accelerate, the robot must lean. The angle per unit of acceleration is set by masses and lengths alone. No controller can reduce it.

Proposition 3 (paper_v2)

Adding the two rows of the equations of motion cancels the torque and the damping (both enter as \(+\) and \(-\)):

$$(M_{11}+M_{12})\ddot\varphi+(M_{12}+M_{22})\ddot\theta-m_alr\sin\theta\,\dot\theta^2=m_agl\sin\theta .$$

Hold a steady lean (\(\ddot\theta=\dot\theta=0\)) and write the ball acceleration as \(a=r\ddot\varphi\). Then \(\sin\theta=\kappa a\) with

$$\kappa=\frac{(m_a+m_k)r^2+I_k+m_alr}{m_a\,g\,l\,r}.$$

The identity has no control term, so it holds for every controller. The paper used it as a check of the inertia model: regression slope 0.88 to 0.93 against the simulator.

Why it also contains the wrong-way start

Right after a torque, before the lean has changed, the right-hand side is still zero, so \(\ddot\varphi\) and \(\ddot\theta\) must have opposite signs.

For the robot on the sliders, \(\kappa\) is ° of lean per m/s². A 20° lean budget caps sustained acceleration near  m/s².

Steady lean needed per acceleration

Slope is κ from the closed form; exact for the linear model. The amber lines mark the 20° budget used by design_constraints.py.

So whatThe governor of section 4 limits acceleration to 0.5 m/s²: about 5° of lean. That is well inside the 15° where the body used to derail off the ball before the arrestors (S79). \(\kappa\) is also scale-free, which is why Froude scaling (section 6) keeps angles unchanged.
4. The LQR

One matrix of gains, and three fixes that make it usable

An LQR turns "how much I dislike each error" into feedback gains. Alone it balances. To follow speed commands it needs three additions: a governor, an integral and a yaw loop.

The gain

Per plane the torque is \(\tau=-Ks\). The weights say what matters:

$$Q=\mathrm{diag}(10^{-6},\,50,\,1,\,1),\qquad R=1 .$$

Almost nothing on ball position, a lot on lean, unit cost on torque.

Details: the state, the gains and the closed-loop poles

The state is \(s=(\varphi,\theta,\dot\varphi-\dot\varphi_{\mathrm{ref}},\dot\theta)\). The continuous-time Riccati equation gives \(K\approx(0,\,76.1,\,4.33,\,11.6)\) in magnitude and closed-loop poles near \(-20.3,-5.02,-4.50\) and 0 (paper_v2). The pale crosses in the section 2 plot are these poles.

Designed at 10 ms, not in continuous time

The controller acts every 10 ms and holds its torque in between. The gain is designed for that sampled system directly. Each robot design gets its own \(K\).

Details: the discrete design
$$\begin{aligned}(A_d,B_d)&=\mathrm{c2d}(A,B,\,\Delta t),\qquad X=\mathrm{DARE}(A_d,B_d,\,Q\Delta t,\,R\Delta t),\\ K&=(R\Delta t+B_d^\top XB_d)^{-1}B_d^\top XA_d .\end{aligned}$$

lqr_gpu.py, design_tables(). One \(K\) is computed per robot design from that design's own \((A,B)\); the earlier CPU study found the continuous design could go unstable under the hold for light rotors, which is why the discrete form is the default here.

The velocity governor: why

A step in commanded speed would demand a torque spike and a large wrong-way reversal. The governor turns the step into a ramp:

$$v_{\mathrm{ref}}\leftarrow v_{\mathrm{ref}}+\Delta v\,\min\!\Big(1,\;\frac{a_{\max}\Delta t}{\lVert\Delta v\rVert}\Big),\qquad a_{\max}=0.5\ \mathrm{m/s^2}.$$

By the lean law that is about 5° of lean. Switch the governor off in the simulator and watch the reversal grow.

The integral: why

The simulator's soft contact creeps, so a pure LQR settles a little off the commanded speed. A slow integral of the velocity error removes the offset:

$$\xi\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}\big(\xi+(v_{\mathrm{ref}}-v_{\mathrm{meas}})\Delta t,\ \pm2\big),\qquad \dot\varphi_{\mathrm{ref}}=\frac{v_{\mathrm{ref}}+k_i\,\xi}{r},\quad k_i=1 .$$

The clip is anti-windup. On flat ground: achieved speed 0.99 to 1.01 of the command (S83).

Yaw and the wheels

Yaw is a third, decoupled channel with a proportional loop, \(\tau_z=-k_{\mathrm{yaw}}(\omega_{\mathrm{cmd}}-\omega_z)\), \(k_{\mathrm{yaw}}=5\). The three torques are mapped to the wheels through the omniwheel transmission \(W\) and clipped to \([-1,1]\).

5. Residual control

Let a learned policy nudge the LQR, but only so far

Keep the LQR. Let a trained policy add a correction. One number, the authority \(\rho\), caps that correction and trades tracking accuracy against robustness.

$$a=\mathrm{clip}\big(a_{\mathrm{LQR}}+\rho\,a_\pi,\ -1,\ 1\big),\qquad a_\pi\in[-1,1]^3 .$$

The policy can move each wheel by at most \(\rho\) of full scale. With \(\rho=0.25\) the LQR keeps at least three quarters of the say. Training starts from the LQR itself.

train_dev.py: applied = (lqr.act() + rs * a).clamp(-1, 1), where rs is the authority; --lqr-base argument.

6. Froude scaling

A bigger robot is the same robot in slow motion

One policy is trained on robots of different sizes. To keep the task fair, speeds, forces and times must scale with size. The rule is Froude scaling.

Derivation in three steps
  1. Scale the geometry. Multiply every length by \(s\): \(r\to sr\), \(l\to sl\). Keep the materials, so masses scale with volume, \(m\to s^3m\), and inertias with mass times length squared, \(I\to s^5I\).
  2. Look for a time scale that keeps the equations the same. Divide the equations of motion by \(m_agl\). Every term becomes a ratio of the form (mass × length²)/(mass × g × length) × (1/time²), that is \(l/(g\,t^2)\). Those ratios are unchanged if \(t\to\sqrt{s}\,t\).
  3. Read off the rest. Speed is length over time: \(v\to\sqrt{s}\,v\). Acceleration is length over time squared: unchanged. Angles: unchanged (so the lean law's \(\kappa\) is the same number for every scale; check it from its formula, numerator and denominator both go as \(s^5\)). Force is mass times acceleration: \(F\to s^3F\). Torque is force times length: \(\tau\to s^4\tau\). The unstable pole is a rate, \(p\to p/\sqrt{s}\): a half-size robot falls \(\sqrt2\) times faster.
$$\frac{v^2}{g\,l}\ \text{(Froude number) invariant}\quad\Rightarrow\quad v\propto\sqrt{s},\quad t\propto\sqrt{s},\quad F\propto s^3,\quad \tau\propto s^4,\quad \theta\ \text{unchanged}.$$

Why commands and pushes are scaled

Command speeds are multiplied by \(\sqrt s\). Pushes scale with weight: the 1.5× design gets 16.9 to 67.5 N instead of 5 to 20 N, the factor \(1.5^3=3.375\). Otherwise a large robot would be tested at a crawl with feeble shoves, and a small one at a sprint.

7. The traction limit at a step edge

How high a step can a ball climb?

A ball pressed against a step can climb it only if friction at the edge can hold the sideways force. That caps the step height at a fixed fraction of the ball radius.

Assumptions

Quasi-staticThe ball creeps over the edge; no momentum. Matches the 0.3 m/s approach of the tests.
RigidA single point contact at the edge.
No wheel slipThe limit comes from the ball-floor contact, not the motors.
Body uprightThe load acts straight down through the ball centre.

Result

$$h_{\max}=r\,\big(1-\cos(\arctan\mu)\big)=r\Big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mu^2}}\Big).$$
Derivation in five steps
  1. Geometry. A ball of radius \(r\) touches a step of height \(h\) at its edge. The line from the edge to the ball centre makes an angle \(\theta\) with the vertical, and the centre sits \(r-h\) above the edge, so
    $$\cos\theta=\frac{r-h}{r}=1-\frac{h}{r}.$$
  2. Lift-off. To climb, the ball must leave the lower floor and pivot on the edge. At that moment the edge is the only support, so it carries the whole vertical load \(W=(m_a+m_k)g\). The edge reaction has a normal part \(N\) along the edge-to-centre line and a friction part \(F\) along the tangent.
  3. Force balance. Quasi-static, so the horizontal forces cancel and the vertical forces carry the weight:
    $$N\sin\theta-F\cos\theta=0,\qquad N\cos\theta+F\sin\theta=W .$$
    The first line gives \(F/N=\tan\theta\): the steeper the contact, the larger the share of the load that friction must carry.
  4. What drives it. The wheel torque on the ball must equal the moment of the edge friction about the centre, \(T=Fr\). The torque is available (no wheel slip, motors far from saturation: under 1 percent in S79), so the only question is whether the edge can hold \(F\).
  5. Coulomb limit. Friction can supply at most \(\mu N\). So climbing is possible only when \(\tan\theta\le\mu\), that is \(\theta\le\arctan\mu\), and therefore
    $$h_{\max}=r\,\big(1-\cos(\arctan\mu)\big)=r\Big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\mu^2}}\Big).$$

With \(\mu\approx1\): \(h_{\max}\approx0.29\,r\). That is 2.6 cm for the 9 cm ball, 4.0 cm for the 13.5 cm ball of the 1.5× design (S88). Simulated floors span friction 0.4 to 1.2, so the limit varies between about 0.07 r and 0.36 r.

Test

One controller, trained only on 9 cm balls, drove at 0.3 m/s at steps and bumps on both balls (S88; MuJoCo-Warp GPU, 640 worlds per kind, undisturbed). On the larger half of each range the 9 cm ball fell 0.98 at step-ups and 0.94 at bumps. The 13.5 cm ball fell 0.80 and 0.62. A perfect height map given to the balance policy did not reduce these falls (S88).

Momentum helps a little. The formula assumes a creeping ball. With a 0.6 m/s run-up the 9 cm ball's limit moves from about 2.8 cm to about 4 cm; at 0.9 m/s the robot itself becomes unstable (S126, g39_goalcentre, three seeds).

Climbable step height

The ball, step and contact angle follow the sliders. The limit does not depend on mass, torque or controller.

So whatAt a slow approach, steps and bumps above \(0.29\,r\) are almost never crossed (S88), and neither a perfect map nor a depth sensor given to the balance policy changes that. Step height is a design decision about ball size and floor friction. Sensing can buy a route around the obstacle: section 9.
8. Ball arrestors

Why holding the body onto the ball leaves the model unchanged

Before the arrestors, 95 percent of falls were the body sliding off the ball at about 15 degrees of tilt, with the motors below 1 percent of their limit (S79). Three small passive rollers stop that, and they do not change the derived model.

The geometry

Each arrestor is an 8 mm sphere fixed to the body, opposite one drive wheel, 100° from the top of the ball (just below the equator). It touches the ball with zero gap and zero preload when the robot stands seated.

retainer.py, settle() and arrestor_positions(). Frictionless by default; mass neglected.

The force argument

  1. Seated, it does nothing. Zero gap, zero preload: no force while the wheels sit on the ball.
  2. Its force is radial. A contact force on a frictionless sphere passes through the centre. It cannot spin the ball.
  3. It is an internal body-ball force. It stops the body lifting or sliding off. No new degree of freedom, no new drive.
  4. Roller friction barely matters. Friction 0, 0.05 and 0.2 gave goal success 0.75, 0.74, 0.76 undisturbed and 0.45, 0.42, 0.42 disturbed (S79).

Measured effect

Added at evaluation to a policy trained without them: goal success rose from 0.57 to 0.75 undisturbed and from 0.28 to 0.45 with pushes and pulls (S79, 100° mount; MuJoCo-Warp GPU, randomisation on, noise on, seed 0).

Details: misalignment and the choice of mount angle

The misalignment at fall dropped from 12 to 19 degrees to 2 to 3 degrees. Mounts between 90 and 100° were equal within noise; 100° was chosen because only a mount below the equator can resist the body lifting off. Floor clearance at 100° is 6.5 cm, touching only beyond 57° of tilt; the diametrically opposite mount at 135° would scrape at 21° and is not buildable on this ball (S79).

So whatThe arrestors improved every robustness measure at once, without retraining and without touching the LQR. Fix with mechanism what mechanism can fix; let learning handle the rest.
9. Navigation

A distance field and a carrot

A block the ball cannot climb must be driven around. The navigation layer only hands the balance controller a velocity command. It uses a grid that knows the distance to the goal, and a follower that aims a little way down that grid.

The wavefront distance field

A \(64\times64\) grid of 5 cm cells surrounds the robot's tile. Ground more than 3 cm above the start is an obstacle, inflated by 0.22 m. The goal cell is 0; every free cell takes one more than its best neighbour:

$$d_{ij}\leftarrow\min\Big(d_{ij},\ 1+\min_{(k,l)\in\mathcal N_8(i,j)} d_{kl}\Big),\qquad d=\infty\ \text{on inflated cells},\quad d_{\mathrm{goal}}=0 .$$

A distance field has no local minima. A local steer-away rule tried first left 0.56 to 0.59 of worlds stuck beside the blocks (S89).

Details: sweeps and update rate

After 110 sweeps the field holds the number of steps to the goal around every obstacle (nav.py, field()). It is recomputed every 0.2 s from the privileged map or from the sensor memory (section 5 of the architecture page).

The carrot follower

The first planner stepped toward the best nearby point every step. It reached the goal 0.91 of the time but its heading jumped about: heading-rate RMS 14.4 rad/s (S89, S90). The smooth follower:

  1. Carrot. Descend the field for 0.3 m from the robot. The point reached is the carrot.
  2. Heading. Turn the commanded heading toward the carrot at most \(w_{\max}=2\) rad/s.
  3. Speed. At most \(v_{\max}=0.3\) m/s, slower near the goal and in sharp turns, changing by at most 0.6 m/s². All speeds are Froude-scaled.

With the privileged map the heading-rate RMS fell from 14.4 to 1.7 rad/s, for a few points of success (0.91 to 0.86) (S90).

Details: the carrot rule inside the margin, and the speed formula

The descent uses 5 cm steps. If the robot stands inside the inflated margin (field \(=\infty\)), the descent starts from the best point on rings 0.1 to 0.3 m around it, or the carrot never leaves the robot (S90). Speed is \(v=\min\big(v_{\max},\ k_{\mathrm{goal}}\,d_{\mathrm{goal}},\ v_{\mathrm{turn}}\big)\) with \(k_{\mathrm{goal}}=1.5\), and \(v_{\mathrm{turn}}\) lowered when the heading error is large. Rate-limited turns clip block corners slightly more often, which is the cost in success.

With a real sensor, aim it

With a forward ToF sensor (18×14 zones) the smooth follower first did worse than the jerky one (0.72 against 0.85): the sensor followed the slowly turning command and saw side blocks late. Aiming it at the planner's target heading recovered most of it: 0.78 success, block contacts 0.16 to 0.03 (S90).

10. Where the model stops being true, and how the page checks itself

Where the model stops being true

Soft contacts

Instantaneous torque-step accelerations match for the ball (3.17 vs 3.01 rad/s² per 0.5 N m) but not for the body (0.73 vs 1.60), because MuJoCo's soft ball-floor contact allows initial creep (paper_v2, validation section). This is what the velocity integral of section 4 mops up.

No traction in the plane

The planar model has no slip and no edges. The traction limit of section 7 is a separate quasi-static argument, not a consequence of the Lagrangian; and the planar torque that friction can carry on flat ground is only about 1.74 N m against \(m_agl=4.94\) N m (design_constraints.csv).

Light balls

The body-to-ball mass ratio is 23 here; the model is least faithful for light balls. The light-ball design kb025 carries nearly all remaining flat-ground falls and LQR gains cannot fix it (S92, S93). See docs/research/design_rules.md.

Checked: poles, zero, lean law, Skogestad floor, LQR closed-loop pole and 3 degree peak torque, every row of the sweep. The LQR is solved in the browser by Newton-Kleinman iteration on the Riccati equation and compared with scipy.linalg.solve_continuous_are through the closed-loop poles and peak torque. Not checked here: the nonlinear RK4 simulator has no Python counterpart in the repo (the full MuJoCo environment is the reference and differs from this planar model, see above); the traction, Froude, authority and wavefront widgets are illustrations of closed-form rules and of the S-numbered measurements, not new computations.
