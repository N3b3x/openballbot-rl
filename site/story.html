The journey

From a robot that barely moves to a controller that knows its own physics

A ballbot balances on one ball and moves only by leaning. This is the story of how we got a small simulated one to go where it is told: twelve chapters, each with the question, what we tried, the data, the decision, and what went wrong. Failures stay in.

Simulation only. Every result on this page comes from the MuJoCo simulator. There is no hardware claim anywhere on this site. Each chapter names the repo file its numbers come from.

    01

    A baseline that barely moves, and a policy that cheats

    S0S1void results

    The question

    Can a learned policy make the ballbot follow a velocity command at 1 m/s, in any direction?

    What we tried

    S0 used the limits from the base repository: wheel torque 10 N m, wheel damping 0.8, a fall declared at 20 degrees of tilt. The robot balanced and barely moved: it achieved only 6% of the commanded speed at 1 m/s. So we gave the policy more authority (S1): torque 30, damping 0.1, tilt limit 70 degrees.

    The reward went up and the speed reached 0.2. It looked like progress.

    The data

    0.06
    S0 speed ratio at 1 m/s
    0.2–0.6 s
    time until the S1 policy had kicked the ball out
    never
    how often the 70-degree tilt check fired
    LimitS0 baselineS1 free
    wheel torque10 N m30 N m
    wheel damping0.80.1
    tilt termination20 deg70 deg

    The decision, and why

    Throw the S1 results away. A trace of an S1 episode showed the policy shoving the ball out from under itself within 0.2 to 0.6 seconds and then standing on its own wheels. The body stayed upright, so the tilt check never fired and the reward kept paying. We added an explicit off-ball termination (max_ball_offset_deg, the angle of the line from ball centre to body from vertical) and logged off_ball_deg in every evaluation.

    The exploit, precisely

    The constraint that defines a ballbot, that it stays on the ball, was never in the reward or the termination. Raising the tilt limit to 70 degrees removed the only proxy for it. The policy found the gap in under a second of simulated time. The paper draft files this under specification gaming (reward misspecification).

    What surprised us

    A policy that looks better on every number you chose to log can be cheating on the one you did not. The lesson recorded in the log is short: check the constraint that defines the task.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (S0, S1); paper_v2/main.tex (related work).

    02

    Staying on the ball: honest balance, smoothness, and reward shaping

    S2S3S4S5

    The question

    With the cheat closed, how fast can a policy go, and which reward makes it go faster?

    What we tried

    • S2, on the ball. Honest balance, speed ratio 0.21 to 0.33. Yaw tracking appeared for the first time.
    • S3, smoothness. An action-rate penalty and a soft ball-offset penalty cut jitter by 8 to 500 times, but speed fell further.
    • S4, lean reward. Pay the policy for leaning into the command while it is below the commanded speed. Best result 0.36.
    • S5, error reward. Reward the tracking error itself, with speed commands up to 1.5 or 2 m/s. Still 0.15 to 0.31, though yaw improved.

    The data

    The decision, and why

    Stop reshaping the reward. Two probes pointed elsewhere. First, the S4 policies used only 1 to 3 percent of available torque and leaned about 1 degree; given a 2 m/s command, the best peaked at 1.21 m/s. That is learned caution, not a physical limit. Second, a fall probe on 30 random-command episodes found that every S2 to S5 policy fell in 20 to 30 of them, and 23 of 29 falls for trk_a (24 of 29 for trk_c) happened under yaw commands above 1 rad/s. Training had sampled yaw up to plus or minus 3 rad/s.

    What failed or surprised us

    • A strong smoothness penalty applied before the policy can move simply freezes it. Order matters: track first, smooth later.
    • A hand-tuned cascaded PD controller, 48 gain and sign combinations, never balanced. Feeding back tilt alone lets the ball run away. We dropped it and decided a proper model-based baseline was owed (chapter 6).

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (S2 to S5).

    03

    Why spin commands above 1 rad/s broke learning

    S6S7S8biggest single jump

    The question

    If the falls cluster on fast spin commands, is the command distribution, not the reward, what limits tracking?

    What we tried

    S6: cap the commanded yaw rate at 1 rad/s and retrain. S7: keep the best policies and refine them, with a four times larger action-rate penalty for trk_h. S8: widen the spin range again to 1.5 rad/s with a curriculum, to see what it costs.

    The data

    0.98
    ratio at 0.5 m/s, trk_f
    0.147 → 0.013
    action rate, trk_g refine, no loss at 0.5 m/s
    3/30
    falls for trk_h, completes the square course

    The decision, and why

    Fix the yaw range at 1 rad/s, and retrain from scratch whenever the command distribution changes. Infeasible commands in training teach the policy that moving is dangerous, which suppresses learning of the feasible ones. The effect is a property of the training distribution, not of the reward shape we had spent three stages tuning. S7 then confirmed the order: track first, smooth afterwards.

    Numbers behind the spin fixes

    Continuing timid policies reached only 0.59, and one collapsed into spinning (30 of 30 falls). In S8 the yaw tracking at plus or minus 1 rad/s improved (spin_a -0.92/+0.76, spin_b -0.89/+0.87, spin_c -0.95/+0.82) but ratios at 1 m/s fell to 0.87, 0.70 and 0.75, against 0.87, 0.81 and 0.95 before. The heading weakness did not change (0.31 to 0.34 m/s).

    What failed or surprised us

    Widening the spin range does not come free: it trades translation for yaw, exactly as the S5 finding predicts. Spin above 1 rad/s stays an open problem in the log.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (S6, S7, S8); paper_v2/main.tex abstract.

    04

    Things that looked wrong: the video, the heading, and the clock

    video fixheadingthroughput

    The question

    Are the weird things we see in the renders failures of the controller, or of our measurement?

    What we tried, and found

    The video artifact. In the renders the robot seemed to wander away from its planned path, as if it had lost the plot. It had not. The 8 second episode limit was silently resetting the robot to its start pose while the commanded path kept going. Videos now disable the time limit, draw the whole planned path (green), the actual path (red) and the target point, stop with a banner on a real fall, and report the distance from the planned point.

    Heading. After a 4 rad turn, a world-frame command was executed in the wrong direction. Every training episode had started with the same heading, so the policy had learned a direction table, not a skill. We added init_yaw_random and a heading test to the benchmark.

    The clock. Profiling showed the Python RK4 contact-frame stepper, four forward passes per physics step, dominated cost. The implicitfast integrator needs one pass and keeps the same contact-frame friction fix.

    The data

    The decision, and why

    Randomise the start yaw in training, test heading explicitly, and use implicitfast from S9 onward. We switched only after showing that policies trained on the slow integrator behave the same on the fast one.

    What surprised us

    Two of the three problems were in our instruments, not in the controller. The heading weakness, though, was real, and it is the reason the later chapters care about heading error as a headline metric.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (Video fix, Heading, Throughput).

    05

    Deriving the physics, then checking it against what the policies learned

    derived modeltheory test

    The question

    The prior report wrote down M q'' + C q' + G = tau without deriving it. What does the actual robot's physics say it can and cannot do?

    What we tried

    We extracted parameters from the simulator model (body 3.853 kg with its centre of mass 0.1306 m above the ball centre, ball 0.168 kg, radius 0.09 m), modelled the omniwheel transmission and the reflected rotor inertia (0.1025 kg m^2, 188 times the ball's own), and wrote the planar Lagrangian. Four things fall out of it.

    • An unstable pole at 4.79 1/s: a fall takes about a fifth of a second to develop.
    • A non-minimum-phase zero at 5.78 1/s, independent of the drive: to go forward you must first push the ball backwards so the body tips forward.
    • A momentum identity: adding the two Lagrange equations cancels torque and damping, so a combination of body and ball motion is conserved by the actuators.
    • A lean law: sin(theta) = kappa a, with kappa = 0.176 rad per m/s^2 (10.1 degrees of lean per m/s^2 of acceleration).
    The equations
    z = sqrt( m_a g l / ( I_a + m_a l^2 + m_a l r ) ) = 5.78 1/s
    p = 4.79 1/s (largest positive eigenvalue of the linearised A)
    sin(theta) = kappa a, kappa = 0.176 rad per m/s^2

    m_a is the body mass, l the centre-of-mass height above the ball centre, r the ball radius, I_a the body pitch inertia (0.0366 kg m^2). The omniwheel transmission W maps ball-relative angular velocity to wheel rates; the planar projection n = (3.61, -1.11, -2.50) gives J_eff = J_w |n|^2. Script: scripts/study/ballbot_model.py.

    The data

    The test of a derived model is whether trained systems obey it. In the full 3-D simulator, the momentum identity held on trajectories of all three controllers (slope 0.93, 0.88, 0.89; R^2 0.82, 0.60, 0.60), and all three showed the predicted non-minimum-phase reversal (1.7, 0.3 and 1.1 cm backwards first).

    The decision, and why

    Treat the derived model as the design basis for everything that follows: a model-based baseline (chapter 6), and a way to read learned behaviour. It earned that trust by predicting data it was not fitted to.

    Caveats we kept

    • Soft contact makes the ball creep at the floor, which corrupts instantaneous-acceleration comparisons.
    • Wheel rates differ from the model's W by a constant 27 to 28 percent (an effective rolling radius), not by a speed-dependent slip.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (First-principles model, Theory vs learned); scripts/study/ballbot_model.py, scripts/study/analyze_theory.py; paper_v2/main.tex.

    06

    LQR: right state, a governor, an integral, and a wall at 1 m/s

    LQR1 m/s edge

    The question

    If the physics is known, how far can a controller designed only on the derived model go, with no simulator tuning?

    What we tried

    A linear quadratic regulator on the derived (A, B), gains of magnitude K = (0, 76.1, 4.33, 11.6) (signs follow the torque convention of the model). Three additions were needed before it worked on the full simulator:

    1. The right state. It must use the ball's rotation as a state. Measured linear velocity of the ball destabilises it, because of the contact creep from chapter 5.
    2. A reference governor. Acceleration commands are limited to 0.5 m/s^2, because the non-minimum-phase zero punishes sudden reference changes.
    3. A slow outer integral on measured velocity, to remove steady-state error.

    The data

    1.00–1.08
    speed ratio at 0.25–0.75 m/s
    0.02
    heading error, m/s
    0.0002
    action rate, about 50× smoother than PPO
    every trial
    falls at 1 m/s, and on the square course's turning leg

    The decision, and why

    Keep the LQR as the accuracy and smoothness reference in every benchmark (lqr). It beats the learned policies at low speed and ignores heading, and it fails exactly where they succeed, which is the argument for combining them.

    The 1 m/s wall: a hypothesis we killed

    Both families fail near 1 m/s. For the LQR we measured: torque below 17 percent of the limit, wheel normal forces steady at 17 to 18 N, no growing slip. Failure coincided with a yaw spin-up and a growing body-ball oscillation. The tempting explanation was 3-D gyroscopic coupling of the wheel rotors (about 0.2 N m s per wheel at 40 rad/s).

    A side-finding on sampling

    A continuous-time LQR with light rotors is unstable once sampled: a fast closed-loop pole at -135 1/s is too fast for 100 Hz. We added discrete_dt (zero-order hold plus the discrete Riccati equation) to the controller. Script: scripts/study/edge_armature.py.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (LQR baseline, 1 m/s edge, edge vs rotor inertia); ballbot_gym/controllers/lqr.py.

    07

    Residual learning on top of the LQR

    S10LQRPPO

    The question

    The LQR is precise but brittle at the edge; PPO is robust but sloppy. Can a small learned correction on top of the LQR keep the first and gain the second?

    What we tried

    residual_lqr: true: the applied action is the LQR action plus beta times the policy output.

    • First attempt (res_a/b: beta 0.3, random initial policy, speeds to 1.5): worse than LQR alone, 25 to 50 percent falls at 0.5 m/s. The untrained residual destabilised the LQR: first episodes lasted about 100 steps versus about 700 for the LQR by itself, and the commands included speeds the LQR cannot survive.
    • Corrected (res_c): zero-initialised output so the starting controller is exactly the LQR (--zero-init-actor), beta 0.1, speeds and spin capped at 1.
    • Bigger (res_d): res_c continued with beta 0.2, domain randomisation and pushes.

    The data

    The decision, and why

    Keep res_c as the residual reference, with zero initialisation and a small beta. A residual with a small beta is enough to shape yaw, where the LQR is weak, but not enough to rescue balance near the edge, where the LQR is simply wrong.

    What failed

    res_c falls 19% of the time at 0.75 m/s and 94% at 1 m/s, and 6 of 10 pushes at 30 N bring it down, where the LQR survives all ten. The residual did not move the LQR's 1 m/s wall, and the bigger one made things worse. (Residual-on-LQR did later turn out best on rough ground: chapter 10.)

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (Residual RL, S10 residual results, res_d).

    08

    Pushes, randomised physics, and the damping brittleness

    S9S11domain randomisation

    The question

    How well do the controllers survive pushes and a robot that is not exactly the one they were trained on?

    What we tried

    A push benchmark (scripts/study/eval_push.py): drive at 0.5 m/s, one 50 ms push at t = 2 s, 10 trials per force, any fall counts. Then a randomised-physics test: mass, wheel friction, wheel damping, torque limit, action latency and sensor noise. Then domain randomisation (DR) in training (S9, S11).

    The data

    Falls of 10, nominal physics40 N push50 N push
    LQR69
    trk_g15
    trk_h00
    rob_a (DR)00

    With randomised physics and no push, trk_g falls 7 of 10, trk_h 6, LQR 4, and the DR-trained rob_a 0. So which parameter hurts?

    The decision, and why

    Fine-tuning a tracking policy under DR works for survival (rob_b: 0 of 10 falls at every nominal push up to 50 N) but brings the timid optimum back. Starting from scratch under the harder distribution did better: mid-training (10.5M of 20M steps) robs_b already had no grid falls and a ratio of 0.48 at 1 m/s. We also narrowed damping to 0.07 to 0.15, a tolerance one can actually measure on a bench.

    What failed or surprised us

    • A first version of the benchmark counted only falls after the push. Fixed.
    • Running more than 10 evaluation processes next to training ran the machine out of memory (OpenBLAS threads). Fix: OPENBLAS_NUM_THREADS=1 and small batches.
    • rob_a_h (fine-tuned from trk_h): ratio 0.29 and some grid falls.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (Robustness benchmark, S9, S11 results).

    09

    The cascade integral: closing the loop around a timid policy

    S12S13PPO + integral

    The question

    If a robust policy tracks only half the commanded speed, can we wrap an outer loop around it so the speed comes out right?

    What we tried

    An outer integral on the velocity error that raises the command the policy sees: c' = c + ki times the integral of (c - v), saturated at the trained range of 1.5 m/s with anti-windup (scripts/study/policy_io.py, run name <run>@ki=<k>). In S13 we also tried putting the integrated error inside the policy as an observation (obs_vint).

    The data

    0.27 → 0.06
    heading error, robs_b without and with the integral (m/s)
    0.045
    heading error, robs_c with the integral: the most accurate robust controller
    0/10
    falls at every nominal push up to 50 N, robs_b@ki=1

    The decision, and why

    Keep the integral as an outer wrapper, saturating at the trained range. The inner policy supplies robustness and the integral supplies accuracy: the same division of labour that the LQR (also with an integral) gets from model knowledge. Putting the integrated error into the observation (robs_c) helped the inner ratio (0.62 and 0.54) and the combination was best.

    What failed or surprised us

    • An earlier saturation at 1.0 m/s was a bug of reasoning: with an inner gain of 0.5 the cap pinned the achieved speed near 0.5.
    • The 1 m/s edge remains: robs_c@ki1 falls 38% of trials at 1.0 m/s, and on the square course the 1 m/s leg was 0.47 short and the robot fell on the turning leg after it.
    • A naive position-feedback loop pushes the speeds to the edge and is listed as an open problem. Velocity tracking does not correct position drift.
    • Training robs_b ten million steps longer made it smoother (action rate 0.0014), not faster.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (S12 cascade, S13 results, Open problems).

    10

    Rough ground, steps, and the traction limit

    PerlinS14S15physical limit

    The question

    Do the controllers cope with uneven floors, and can they climb a small step, the way a book on a floor would demand?

    What we tried

    Perlin terrain without terrain training (4 and 8 cm amplitude). At 0.5 m/s all but trk_h track within 0.02 m/s. At 0.75 m/s res_c had no falls on either amplitude, the LQR 12 to 25 percent, robs_b and robs_c with the integral 12 to 75 percent. Residual-on-LQR generalises best to rough ground.

    S14, terrain training (random Perlin 0 to 10 cm): it did not help. res_t was about equal to res_c, and robs_t with the integral degraded with amplitude (62 to 88 percent falls at 12 cm).

    Step edges (eval_step.py): a raised floor at y = 1 m with a 2 cm bevel; success means cross, stop, and hold for 2 seconds. An early version counted success at the crossing and used identical trials; both were fixed.

    S15, step training on book terrain (steps 0 to 4 cm, commands within 45 degrees of straight ahead at 0.3 to 1.0 m/s): climb_a from robs_c, climb_b from res_c.

    The data

    For the LQR, a speed sweep shows a climb window: minimum speeds of about 0.3, 0.4, 0.5 and 0.5 to 0.6 m/s for 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 cm steps, failure above about 0.8 m/s. The minimum sits below sqrt(2 g h) because the motors add energy.

    The decision, and why

    Stop training for steps: the limit is physical, not learned. A static estimate says the horizontal force needed to roll the ball over a step is F = W tan(alpha) with cos(alpha) = (r - h)/r.

    The design implication for a small real robot: a larger ball relative to step height, more wheel preload, higher-friction wheels.

    What failed or surprised us

    • Terrain training and step training both failed to move the limit. Both are kept in the dead-ends list.
    • The earlier book study, in the original simulator envelope, never crossed 1 cm.
    • The frozen depth features from the two ball-facing cameras were ignored by the policy (blank depth changed nothing), because those cameras mostly see the ball itself. This is why the roadmap moved to side-mounted time-of-flight sensors.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (Perlin terrain, S14, Step edges, S15, Traction hypothesis); docs/research/ballbot_v2_roadmap.md section 1.

    Testing the literature's claims on our own robot

    S161 supported2 refuted

    The question

    A survey of ballbot papers (see the literature page) suggested why our LQR hits a wall and why small robots fail. Do those explanations hold in our simulator?

    What we tried

    Hypothesis from the literatureTestVerdict
    Control rate is the limitLQR on nominal and half-scale robots at 100, 250 and 500 Hzrefuted identical falls at 1.0 m/s and for s = 0.5; consistent with a delay bound exp(pT) of about 1.1 at 10 ms
    Friction is the limitwheel-ball cross friction x2, x4; roll friction x10refuted the LQR's 1 m/s limit and the s = 0.5 failure do not move; PPO tracks 1 m/s (0.91 to 0.95), so the limit belongs to the linear controller or planar model
    Steeper wheel contact angle helps (micro-ballbot paper and patent: 60 to 70 deg zenith; ours 51)design parameter dz rotates each wheel assembly about the ball centre; LQR pushed with 1 x body weight, 6 trials per cellsupported see chart

    The data

    The decision, and why

    Do not chase control rate or wheel friction; treat the wheel contact angle as a first-class design variable. The planar model predicts no effect on traction-limited torque (the extra normal force N ~ 1/cos(zeta) is cancelled by the shorter lever arm), so here the simulator disagrees with the theory and the model misses a mechanism.

    Also tested

    A more realistic actuator (env.motor: first-order current lag, back-EMF torque-speed curve, Coulomb friction; 150 rad/s no-load, 3 N m stall, 50 mN m friction, 3 ms lag) makes the nominal LQR lose 0.8 m/s tracking (falls 4 of 4), yet it still survives pushes of 1 x weight (6 of 6). Lesson: controllers must be retrained with the actuator model in the loop.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (S16 tests of the literature hypotheses); docs/research/design_rules.md.

    12

    The design-space study and the interleaved hybrid controller

    S16gated hybridstill training

    The question

    Given what works where (LQR for accuracy, robust PPO for pushes, residual-on-LQR for rough ground), can one pipeline combine them, and how do the physical choices (scale, ball mass, body mass, centre-of-mass height, motors, rotor inertia) change what is achievable and learnable?

    What we tried

    Design space. A design is (s, kball, kbal, zb, J, tau, damp): geometric scale, ball-mass multiplier, ballast multiplier, ballast height, rotor inertia, wheel torque, damping. For each variant the tool re-derives the planar model and reports the pole, the zero, a sensitivity bound, the lean law, torque authority and acceleration limits (scripts/study/design_space.py).

    Hybrid. The policy outputs three wheel actions plus a gate logit; the applied action is (1 - g) times the LQR action plus g times the PPO action, with g between 0 and 1. It observes the LQR action and a 4-number morphology context, and each reset draws a design from a pool, so one policy meets a family of ballbots. The output layer starts at zero with gate bias -0.8 (g = 0.1), so training starts from almost-LQR and the cascade integral can still wrap it. The residual of chapter 7 is the special case a_pi = a_LQR + beta u.

    The data

    Other analytic findings: p scales as s to the minus one half (a half-size robot must be controlled sqrt(2) faster); the lean constant stays between 7.3 and 14.8 deg per m/s^2 over the whole sweep; the LQR-recoverable linear tilt falls below 20 degrees at a torque authority of about 6.9 (tau of 6 N m per wheel at s = 1).

    Zero-shot LQR (model re-derived per variant, no training) holds 9 of 13 designs without a fall at the slowest test speed (4 of 13 at 0.8 of the scaled speed) and fails at once on the half-scale robot and the 0.25-times ball. The planar model is least faithful when the ball is much lighter than the body (mass ratio above about 60).

    The decision, and why

    Interleave rather than choose. Each family beat the other somewhere: the LQR wins accuracy and smoothness below 0.75 m/s, PPO survives the nonlinear regime (1 m/s) and pushes, the residual wins on rough floors. A learned gate lets the data decide, and starting at g = 0.1 keeps the safety of the model while it learns. The design rules that fell out of the sweep are summarised in docs/research/design_rules.md: a structural sensitivity floor (about 20 dB), a traction limit of about 0.31 N m per wheel (the 30 N m motor limit has a 133 times margin), and a delay budget of about 145 ms.

    What we cannot say yet

    No final hybrid benchmark exists in the log. The gated controller and the morphology pool are built and training; the tracking log records no finished head-to-head against LQR, robs_c@ki or res_c. Until that table exists, the hybrid is a design with a rationale, not a result. The literature search found no prior LQR/RL gate for a balancing robot, but that was one search pass, so we claim novelty cautiously.

    Source: docs/research/tracking_study_log.md (S16); docs/research/design_rules.md; scripts/study/design_space.py, design_constraints.py, eval_design.py.
