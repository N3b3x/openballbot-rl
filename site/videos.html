Watch it balance, and watch it fall

Thirty-three clips from the corrected engine, in six chapters that follow the study: why the robot fell and what three small rollers changed, the controller recommended at the time and its known weakness, why obstacle size and not speed decides a fall, how the ball radius sets the step a robot can climb, what seeing the terrain buys when the obstacle can be avoided, and the motion-layer controller (g34), compared with g40.

Chapter 1

Why it fell, and the arrestors

Before this chapter the robot reached its goal about 0.58 of the time. The fall diagnosis (S79) found that the motors were almost never the limit (torque saturation below 1 percent) and the body rarely tipped past 70 degrees: in 95 percent of falls the body derailed off the ball, at a median tilt of about 15 degrees, because nothing but gravity and three wheel contacts held the two together. Three passive 8 mm rollers mounted at 100 degrees polar angle, touching the ball only at the loaded seat, were added in the model. Given to the policy below without any retraining they lift goal success from 0.57 to 0.75 undisturbed and from 0.28 to 0.45 with pushes and pulls (S79). The pairs below are the same policy in the same world, arrestors off and on.

Chapter 2

The recommended controller

With the arrestors in place the question became how much the learned part should be allowed to override the LQR. The answer from the authority sweep (S82, S83) is 0.25: the applied action is the LQR action plus a quarter of the policy's action, clipped. That keeps speed tracking exact (achieved 0.99 to 1.01 of the command) and goal success at 0.71 to 0.76 undisturbed and 0.42 to 0.44 with pushes and pulls, on three seeds. One clip of it doing its job, and one honest clip of where it does not.

Chapter 3

Obstacle size, not speed

A first analysis suggested that slowing down would get the robot over obstacles; it was withdrawn when 8 s episodes turned out to be too short for slow robots to reach the feature at all (S86, S87). With 20 s episodes the fall rate is the same at 0.1, 0.2 and 0.3 m/s, and what decides it is size: up-steps and bumps above about 2.4 cm, a quarter of the ball radius, fail 90 to 95 percent of the time. A driven ball can only climb an edge while friction can supply the tangential force, which gives h_max = r (1 - cos(arctan mu)), about 0.29 r: 2.6 cm for this 9 cm ball (S88). The two clips below bracket that limit with the same controller and the same 0.3 m/s command.

Chapter 4

Ball size sets the step size

The traction model says the largest climbable step scales with the ball: h_max about 0.29 r, so 2.0 cm for a 6.75 cm ball, 2.6 cm for 9 cm, 3.9 cm for 13.5 cm and 5.2 cm for 18 cm. To test it with one controller, the recommended recipe was trained on the 11 designs plus the robot at 0.75, 1.5 and 2 times nominal size (policy g24_scale_rs025_s0, commands Froude-scaled). Over 640 worlds per kind and size, the 2.3 to 5.9 cm step band is fatal for the 6.75 cm ball (1.00), mostly fatal for 9 cm (0.95) and survivable for 13.5 and 18 cm (0.69, 0.58) (S95). The first pair puts the smallest and the largest trained robot in front of the same 5 cm step; the three clips after it give each robot a step near its own limit. Full tables: generalist study, chapter 9.

Chapter 5

Terrain-aware navigation

If the small ball cannot climb an 8 cm block, the only option is to go around it. On a map where every start is boxed in by 8 cm blocks (S89), the balance controller is frozen and a planner above it feeds the velocity commands. What changes between the three clips is only where the obstacle information comes from: nowhere, a perfect height map, or a simulated 18 x 14 zone time-of-flight sensor on a gimballed head mount, 25 degrees down. Across 528 worlds goal success is 0.09, 0.91 and 0.85 respectively (S89).

Chapter 6

Motion layer and adaptive authority (g34, and g40 for comparison)

The clips in this chapter use the controller recommended from S113 to S120: the LQR plus a learned residual whose authority switches between 0.15 and 0.3, trained and run with a motion layer that smooths commands, brakes into the goal and holds position (checkpoint g34_mref_rsad_db_s0, S112 and S113). Randomisation and sensor noise are on in every clip. Since this chapter was written the recommendation has moved to g39 (goal logic on the true ball centre, S121), and the last pair of clips below compares g34 with g40, the same recipe plus a 240 ms memory.
